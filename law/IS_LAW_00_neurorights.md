# Neurorights and the law

## What neurorights are

Neurorights are proposed human rights that protect the mind from neurotechnology: devices that read or change the activity of the brain and nervous system, and the AI that interprets what they record. The idea is that existing rights to privacy and bodily integrity were written for a world in which no one could reach inside a person's thoughts, and may not be enough now that devices can.

There is no single agreed list. The two most cited are:

- **The NeuroRights Foundation** (founded by the neuroscientist Rafael Yuste) proposes five rights:
  - **Mental privacy:** data from the brain is kept private and is not used or sold without consent.
  - **Personal identity:** technology does not alter a person's sense of self.
  - **Free will:** people make their own decisions, without manipulation by neurotechnology.
  - **Fair access to mental augmentation:** technology that enhances the mind is shared fairly.
  - **Protection from bias:** algorithms used in neurotechnology do not discriminate.
- **Marcello Ienca and Roberto Andorno** (*Life Sciences, Society and Policy*, 2017) propose four:
  - **Cognitive liberty:** the right to use, or refuse, technology that alters the mind.
  - **Mental privacy:** protection of information from the brain.
  - **Mental integrity:** protection from harmful interference with the brain.
  - **Psychological continuity:** protection of a person's sense of being the same person over time.

Neuroethics is the wider field that asks these questions; neurorights are the part of it that tries to turn the answers into law.

## Are neurorights protected today? How would we know?

**Would we know?** This page asks the question plainly, because the honest answer is: mostly, no.

What the law says is the easy part, and the rest of this page sets it out. Whether it is upheld is harder. Several U.S. states now treat neural data as sensitive data, Chile has written the protection of brain activity into its constitution, and international bodies have adopted principles. But:

- **The U.S. state laws are consumer-privacy laws.** They govern businesses that handle consumers' data. Colorado's exempts state and local government by name. Only Montana's limits government, by requiring a warrant or subpoena. See *Neural data held by government* in the United States section.
- **Enforcement is public only when it happens.** Only the attorney general (and, in Colorado, district attorneys) can enforce these laws; individuals cannot sue under them. As of 11 November 2026 we have found **no published enforcement action** under any U.S. state neural-data law. That is a statement about what we found, not proof that none exists.
- **There is no crime called "neurological crime".** Harm done with neurotechnology would be prosecuted, if at all, under general laws: stalking and harassment, wiretapping, computer intrusion, and, for people acting under official authority, the federal civil-rights crimes (18 U.S.C. §§ 241 and 242). Records of such cases rarely say a neurotechnology was involved.
- **One court has ruled.** In August 2023 Chile's Supreme Court ordered the company Emotiv to delete the brain data its device had collected from a former senator, finding the data had been kept without valid consent, and asked Chile's public-health and customs authorities to check that the device's sale and use comply with the law. It is the clearest example we have found of a neurorights protection being enforced by a court.

**What would show it:**

- attorney-general enforcement announcements and settlements (in Colorado, [coag.gov/cpa](https://coag.gov/cpa));
- Federal Trade Commission orders about health and biometric data;
- court records (in the United States, [PACER](https://pacer.uscourts.gov) and [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com));
- data-protection authority decisions abroad;
- criminal prosecutions under stalking, wiretap and civil-rights laws that describe the technology used.

The News on this site follows these sources. When one of them shows a neural-data law being enforced, it will appear in the News and in the entry for that law.

## When a privacy law does not apply, criminal law still does

Several neural-data laws exempt government handling of data. That exemption is about data-handling rules. It does not make crimes lawful. In the United States, harassment and stalking are crimes for anyone, including people working for a government; it is a federal crime for two or more people to conspire to injure or intimidate someone in exercising their rights (18 U.S.C. § 241), and for anyone acting under official authority to deprive a person of their rights (18 U.S.C. § 242); and people can sue officials who violate their rights under state authority (42 U.S.C. § 1983).

## Three ways of governing AI

The law professor Nita Farahany describes the European Union, the United States and China as pursuing different goals rather than one race ([*Thinking Freely*, 1 December 2025](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/chinas-ai-governance-inside-my-ai)):

- **The European Union sets the floor.** It regulates rights first and sorts AI by use: some uses are banned, some are high-risk and heavily regulated, others carry transparency duties. Its rules spread because companies that sell in Europe follow them everywhere.
- **The United States holds the frontier.** It relies on technological leadership, export controls, sector regulators and executive orders rather than one federal AI law, and the federal government is pushing back against state AI laws.
- **China makes itself unavoidable.** It governs AI through foundational data laws, targeted rules for each technology and technical standards, with model registration and real-name traceability, while offering free AI models abroad and controlling key materials.
