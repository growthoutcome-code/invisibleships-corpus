# The Brain: United States

There is no federal law on AI or on neural data. Federal policy is set mostly by executive orders, which direct federal agencies but do not create rights for individuals. States have passed their own laws.

## Federal

### Executive Order 14365, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence"
- **Status:** signed 11 December 2025.
- **What it does:** directs the Commerce Department to list state AI laws it considers burdensome, ties some broadband funding to that list, sets up a Justice Department AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state laws in court, asks the FCC and FTC to consider federal rules that would override state ones, and calls for federal legislation.
- **What it means for a person there:** state protections may be challenged in court or pressured through funding. They remain law unless a court or Congress says otherwise.
- **Analysis:** Nita Farahany (*Thinking Freely*, 12 December 2025) sets out the constitutional arguments on each side and notes that the Senate voted 99 to 1 in 2025 to remove a similar ten-year pause on state AI laws from the budget bill.
- **Sources:** the order (govinfo.gov); Baker Botts, "Inside the DOJ's New AI Litigation Task Force".

### Executive Order 14409, "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security"
- **Status:** signed 2 June 2026; published in the Federal Register on 5 June 2026.
- **What it covers:** security testing and coordination for the most capable AI models, including risks related to cyberattacks and to chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear weapons.
- **Analysis:** Nita Farahany (*Thinking Freely*, 26 October 2026) describes the testing as voluntary and argues that every proposed safeguard against AI-assisted weapons leads back to more surveillance, first of AI models and then of people.
- **Sources:** Federal Register document 2026-11415.

### Executive order "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence"
- **Status:** signed 29 September 2026; Federal Register document 2026-20321.
- **What it does:** directs the executive branch to use "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in place of "Artificial Intelligence" and "AI". A voluntary "White House Accord on Super Intelligence" was signed at the same time with leaders of AI companies.
- **Analysis:** Nita Farahany (*Thinking Freely*, 2 October 2026): neither this order nor California's response the next day creates enforceable rights.
- **Sources:** whitehouse.gov (the order and its fact sheet).

### Thought and the Fifth Amendment
The Fifth Amendment protects a person from being forced to testify against themselves: what comes from the mind, not physical evidence such as blood or fingerprints. Nita Farahany (*Thinking Freely*, 30 March 2026) explains why devices that read intention before a person moves, and AI conversation logs that record thinking in progress, strain that line, and that the courts have not settled it. Cases: *Schmerber v. California* (1966), *Fisher v. United States* (1976), *United States v. Hubbell* (2000).

## Colorado

### Colorado Privacy Act: neural data (HB24-1058)
- **Status:** signed 17 April 2024; in effect since 6 August 2024.
- **What it covers:** adds biological and neural data (information from measuring the activity of a person's central or peripheral nervous system) to "sensitive data". Covered businesses need a person's **opt-in consent** before processing it and must assess the risks.
- **Who has to comply:** businesses handling the data of 100,000 or more Colorado residents a year, or 25,000 or more while making money from selling data.
- **Government use:** **exempt** for data kept by the State, the courts, counties, cities and towns, and state colleges and universities, when collected and used as authorised by law for noncommercial purposes. A state law generally does not reach federal agencies.
- **Enforcement:** the Attorney General and district attorneys; up to $20,000 per violation. Individuals cannot sue under it.
- **What it does not cover:** government use as above; what AI concludes from neural data; phones and other devices that infer a person's mental state from typing, voice or sleep.
- **Analysis:** Nita Farahany (*Thinking Freely*, 5 August 2025) writes that the neural-data laws of California, Colorado, Montana and Connecticut protect the raw signal from the nervous system, not what AI infers from it.
- **Sources:** C.R.S. 6-1-1303 and 6-1-1304.

### Automated Decision-Making Technology Act (SB 26-189)
- **Status:** signed 14 May 2026; takes effect 1 January 2027 if the Attorney General completes the required rules. It replaced Colorado's 2024 AI Act before that law took effect, after an AI company sued to block the 2024 law and the federal government moved to join the suit; the Attorney General agreed to pause enforcement while the case and the rewrite were pending.
- **What it covers:** telling people when automated technology is used in important decisions about them; a right to ask for meaningful human review after a decision goes against them; disclosures within 30 days. It drops the 2024 law's duty to take reasonable care against algorithmic discrimination.
- **Enforcement:** the Attorney General only; up to $20,000 per violation, with 60 days to fix a problem first.
- **Sources:** Skadden, "Colorado Repeals and Replaces Its AI Act" (June 2026).
