## Captured text

The most formidable barrier in medicine is the one that separates the brain from the bloodstream. Some small, simple chemical compounds such as paracetamol can cross the blood–brain barrier, but for larger molecules, such as proteins and messenger RNA, it is completely impermeable. There is a good reason for this — toxins and pathogens swimming in the blood could wreak havoc in the brain. But the barrier also makes it difficult to treat the devastating diseases that affect this delicate organ.

Neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s affect more than 57 million people worldwide. Brain tumours are among the deadliest forms of cancer — people diagnosed with the most common type, glioblastoma, survive on average for less than 15 months. Therapies have been in development for decades, but getting them into the brain has proved to be a major obstacle.

Strategies that bypass the blood–brain barrier, such as intracranial injections, are invasive and risky. Some researchers think that small particles that are engineered to exploit natural transport mechanisms in the brain could be used to smuggle therapeutic cargoes through the barrier and allow large medicinal molecules to access the organ non-invasively.

Lipid nanoparticles are being developed to ferry gene-based therapeutics, such as those involving mRNA, into the brain. Researchers are also studying polymer nanoparticles, for which almost any property can be tuned to suit the requirements. And some scientists are exploring the possibility of using particles that are naturally secreted from cells, called exosomes, as cross-border drug carriers to avoid unwanted immune attention. Each of these approaches is inching towards human trials; should they continue to advance, stern regulatory and scalability challenges await.

## **Smuggling cargoes**

The blood–brain barrier is a complex cellular structure wrapped around vasculature throughout the brain. Its main component is a layer of brain endothelial cells, connected by protein complexes and appropriately named tight junctions. Cells called pericytes provide structural support and control blood flow, and astrocytes regulate waste clearance and immune responses (see ‘A formidable obstacle’).

The barrier controls the passage of nutrients, such as glucose, fatty acids and amino acids, into the brain; removes waste products; and keeps out toxins, pathogens and, unfortunately, most drugs. Tiny molecules such as water can slip between the cells, and small, fat-soluble molecules sometimes simply diffuse through them. Everything else must travel through one of a few established mechanisms.

The best understood, and most frequently targeted, of these mechanisms is receptor-mediated transcytosis. Certain molecules, known as ligands, bind to specific receptors on brain endothelial cells and trigger a process called endocytosis, in which the cell engulfs the receptor, the ligand and anything else that might be attached. The cell membrane bulges inwards, budding off into compartments called vesicles, which can then exit through the other side of the cell, into the brain.

The most thoroughly studied example is the transferrin receptor, which transports iron into the brain. Others include LRP1, which clears toxins from the brain, and insulin receptors.

Researchers have had most success in harnessing this process by using antibodies as ligands to ferry an attached drug across the barrier. In 2021, for example, Japanese regulators approved a system that uses the transferrin receptor to deliver an enzyme to the brain for the treatment of Hunter syndrome, a rare genetic disorder; the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) cleared a similar therapy in March. Researchers are also testing antibodies that can not only trigger receptor-mediated transcytosis, but also bind to disease-relevant targets once they are inside the brain. One such ‘bispecific’ antibody, called trontinemab, binds to the transferrin receptor and is in phase III clinical trials for Alzheimer’s disease.

Peptides, too, can trigger receptor-mediated transcytosis. A treatment consisting of an engineered peptide called angiopep-2, which binds to LRP1 to transport a chemotherapy drug across the blood–brain barrier, is being tested in people with cancer that has spread to the brain.

But both strategies have a crucial problem: antibodies and peptides carry a very limited payload. Each ligand transfers drug molecules across the blood–brain barrier on an essentially one-to-one basis, and those molecules cannot be too complex. “Antibody technologies are fine, but there are limitations,” says Willard Costain, a pharmacologist at the National Research Council of Canada in Ottawa. “Nanoparticles allow you to do things that antibodies attached to a protein simply can’t.”

## **Small-scale solutions**

For a nanoparticle to trigger receptor-mediated transcytosis, ligands must be attached to its surface. To minimize toxicity, the chosen ligands should enable transport into the brain and very little else. “You need selectivity for the blood–brain barrier, versus the periphery,” says Umar Iqbal, a nanotechnologist at the National Research Council of Canada. Furthermore, because the expression of receptors on brain endothelial cells can be affected by disease type or stage, different treatments might need different ligands. “It’s not one strategy fits all,” says Cláudia Martins, a biomedical scientist at the University of Porto, Portugal. “It’s really disease dependent.”

One nanoparticle can carry multiple ligands on its surface — one to cross the blood–brain barrier and another to get transported inside target brain cells afterwards, for example. “When you use a protein, or especially an RNA, you need these systems to penetrate the cell,” says Cristina Fornaguera, a bioengineer at Ramon Llull University in Barcelona, Spain.

Nanoparticles are also often coated with chemicals called surfactants to stabilize them, stop them aggregating and shield them from the immune system. For example, a coating of polyethylene glycol (PEG) enables a nanoparticle to circulate in the blood for longer before attracting the attention of the immune system, giving it more opportunity to engage with the blood–brain barrier.

In most studies, researchers identify the pathway across the blood–brain barrier that they want to use, attach an appropriate targeting ligand to the nanoparticle of choice, and then measure whether it gets into the brain. If it works, few studies try to confirm that the intended pathway is the one being used. “You hypothesize this will be the route, but you cannot ensure your particle uses exclusively this,” Fornaguera says.

Some researchers have now begun to screen large numbers of candidate particles and look for what sticks. “People administer a lot of particles, then see what crosses the blood–brain barrier,” says Habib Baghirov, a drug-delivery researcher at Åbo Akademi University in Turku, Finland. This ‘directed evolution’ approach was an important factor in the advancement of viral vectors that deliver genetic material into cells, Baghirov says. “With a lag of several years, the nanoparticle field is catching up.”

In October, researchers led by Yizhou Dong, an immunologist at Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York City, used screening to derive a lipid nanoparticle (LNP) for delivering mRNA therapies to the brain<sup>[1](https://www.nature.com#ref-CR1)</sup>. Dong and his colleagues selected six molecules that had previously been shown to cross the blood–brain barrier, then attached them to a variety of different LNP formulations to create 72 candidates. After iterative testing and tuning, they identified ‘MK16’ as the most successful.

Unusually, Dong also wanted to find out how the nanoparticle gets into the brain. “A focus for our group is trying to understand how these particles interact with the blood–brain barrier,” he says. By blocking specific pathways and monitoring whether MK16 made it through, the team found evidence that the nanoparticle makes use of two uncommon mechanisms (see ‘Roads less travelled’).

The first is a form of receptor-mediated transcytosis called the caveolae pathway. “Caveolae has been recognized as a mechanism, but it’s less well-understood,” Costain says. The other involves a membrane-bound enzyme called γ-secretase, which Dong’s group was the first to associate with getting molecules across the blood–brain barrier. “We think it played an important role for our particles,” he says.

## **Positively elegant**

As well as their high capacity, nanoparticles also protect fragile cargoes. Naked mRNA would quickly be destroyed by enzymes in the blood, but loading MK16 with mRNA therapies and injecting it intravenously showed benefits in mouse models of glioblastoma and addiction. Dong and his colleagues also confirmed that it delivered mRNA into cells in human brain-tissue samples.

The technology the team used is known as ionizable cationic LNPs — the same technique that made the COVID-19 mRNA vaccines possible. Most biological lipids have no charge. Cationic lipids, however, are positively charged, and so bind strongly to negatively charged genetic material. This allows these LNPs to load genetic therapies very efficiently. “Lipid nanoparticles are excellent vehicles for genetic drugs,” Martins says.

Simple cationic lipids would have a major downside: they stick to negatively charged blood proteins and disrupt cell membranes. But the ‘ionizable’ LNPs that Dong uses are pH-sensitive and lose their charge in the slightly alkaline bloodstream, sidestepping such toxic effects.

This pH sensitivity also confers other capabilities on the particle. Once inside a brain cell, nanoparticles end up in acidic vesicles called endosomes and become positively charged. This causes the endosomes to rupture, releasing the payload into the cell, where it can get to work. “It’s very elegant,” Iqbal says. “That advance has really changed the game.”

The ability to respond to stimuli is also a feature of many polymer nanoparticles (PNPs) under development. In early work, polymers were engineered with bonds that break in acidic tumour environments, causing the particle to fall apart and dump therapies on cancer cells. In 2023, Martins and her colleagues described a multifunctional PNP that exploits pH sensitivity to cross the blood–brain barrier and then target cancer cells<sup>[2](https://www.nature.com#ref-CR2)</sup>.

The researchers designed a particle based on a biodegradable synthetic polymer called PLGA, which has been used in surgical sutures since the 1970s. To this core, they then added two ‘arms’ of different lengths, made of PEG. To the longer arm, the team attached angiopep-2; to the shorter arm, a ligand called l-histidine.

The two-phase delivery system works in an innovative way. First, the long PEG arm shields the particle in the bloodstream, allowing angiopep-2 to bind to LRP1 on endothelial cells and trigger receptor-mediated endocytosis. Inside the cell, the acidic environment of the endosome breaks a pH-sensitive chemical link, shedding angiopep-2. The remaining particle then exits the cell, into the brain.

The second ligand, which is now better exposed, having shed the longer arm, can then bind to its receptor: a membrane transporter called LAT1 that is overexpressed on glioblastoma cells compared with healthy brain cells. This triggers endocytosis into the cancer cells, where the particle dumps its chemotherapy drug.

In Martins’ study, injecting the nanoparticle into mouse models of glioblastoma increased median survival time from 37 days to 54 days. Removing any component of the nanoparticle reduced the survival gains, suggesting that each part is important to its performance.

These results are promising, but there are major caveats. Mice with artificially induced tumours bear little resemblance to people with cancer. “There are biological differences, such as circulation times, immune responses, receptor expression and tissue distances,” says Martins. As a result, “many nanoparticles that work in rodents fail in larger animals”, she says.

Even if the system proves effective in more realistic models, there are other challenges. “The nanoparticle we developed is complex and heavily chemically modified,” Martins says. This increases cost and creates greater regulatory complexity — and PNPs face a tough road to approval as it is. “Every time we use a different polymer matrix, it’s considered a new chemical entity,” Martins says. This is not the case for LNPs. “Lipids have already been approved by regulatory agencies, so the path to the clinic for new formulations is easier,” Fornaguera says.

Partly for this reason, Dong and his colleagues’ LNP is expected to enter human testing first. “We’re working with clinicians and already engaged with the FDA,” Dong says. “We hope to initiate first-in-human studies in the next couple of years.”

## **Nature’s own nanoparticles**

Despite the potentially greater difficulty in obtaining regulatory go-ahead, some researchers consider PNPs more versatile than their lipid counterparts. LNPs invariably consist of specific components, such as phospholipids, cholesterol and PEG. “The freedom to operate is very limited,” says Fornaguera. Synthetic polymers such as PLGA, by contrast, are easily modified and endlessly tunable — features such as size, shape, structure, stability and charge can all be tailored to requirements. “Polymers are synthesized from scratch, so you can customize your systems more,” Fornaguera says.

The properties of a PNP might, for example, be tuned to adjust for clearance by the liver and spleen, immune reactions and, of course, penetration of the blood–brain barrier. “Nanoparticles can change the drug’s entire pharmacology,” Martins says. “We can even use them to combine different types of cargo.”

But one notable problem affects both polymer and lipid nanoparticles: their biocompatibility. Both forms of nanoparticle can sometimes provoke immune reactions, which can cause harm owing to inflammation. Countermeasures such as PEG coatings can reduce the effect, but some researchers think that a third type of tiny cargo-carrying particle could solve it entirely.

They are betting on exosomes: extracellular vesicles (EVs) that are produced when cells spit out endosomes. Exosomes are natural secretions, which mainly facilitate cell-to-cell communication, and therefore don’t provoke adverse immune reactions. They also cross cellular barriers with ease.

The problem for researchers seeking to load them with therapeutics and aim them at the brain is that exosomes are more difficult than even complex PNPs to produce in large numbers with any consistency. Early efforts involved incubating huge numbers of cells for days. But the exosomes produced were highly variable and loading them with mRNA was difficult to achieve at scale.

In 2020, a team led by James Lee, a bioengineer at the Ohio State University in Columbus, made a leap forward using a technique called cellular nanoporation<sup>[3](https://www.nature.com#ref-CR3)</sup>. Lee and his colleagues developed a biochip filled with tiny channels and loaded with DNA. Culturing cells on these chips and then zapping them with a small current shocked the cells into opening pores, allowing the DNA to flow in.

The cells transcribed the DNA and packed the resulting mRNA into vesicles. Over the following hours, the cells spat out exosomes more rapidly than Lee had ever seen, each containing thousands of times more mRNA than had previously been achieved. “We were quite surprised,” says Lee.

The mRNA in Lee’s exosomes encoded a tumour-suppressing protein called PTEN. By engineering cancer-targeting peptides onto the exosomes, the team were able not only to get the genetic cargo into the brain, but also to direct it to tumours.

Exosomes are not yet fully understood, and delivering them to specific cells might pose safety issues. Pharmaceutical “companies raised many concerns when they realized it was impossible to determine in detail what was inside”, Fornaguera says. Lee concedes that, compared with lipid and polymer nanoparticles, exosomes are more complicated and will require robust quality control and scalable manufacturing to reach the clinic. But “if we can gain better control over surface targeting and cargo loading, engineered EVs have tremendous potential”, he says.

Opinions differ on the relative merits of exosomes, LNPs and PNPs. “A brain-targeted nanoparticle hasn’t yet reached the market, so we don’t know which will perform best,” says Fornaguera. “Maybe a hybrid system would be ideal.” Computational models for predicting nanoparticles’ behaviour could accelerate progress. Researchers also need better models of the blood–brain barrier itself, Fornaguera adds.

Which approach will overcome the obstacle of the blood–brain barrier — if any succeed — remains to be seen. But those working on the problem are confident that the solution will come. “Twenty years ago, antibodies were experimental, so who knows what the standard platform will be 20 years from now,” Baghirov says. “It’s all a matter of time.”
