## Assisted suicide rises in Hawaii amid opposition

| **Getting your [Trinity Audio](http://trinityaudio.ai) player ready...** | 

Carla Fong’s uncle had struggled with serious health problems for over two decades that repeatedly sent him to the hospital and kept him from living the life he wanted.

When his wife died, he was so ill that he could not even leave the hospital to attend her funeral.

Then, at 71, he was diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer. Already weakened by years of underlying health conditions, his health deteriorated further.

Fong remembers her uncle telling her and her parents that he felt he was missing out on life.

“We almost felt like it was so selfish of us for wanting him to stay and fight when he was just so tired,” she said.

He lost significant weight, became increasingly frail and pale, and his personality changed. Hospital stays became more frequent, medical bills continued to mount, and he grew increasingly unhappy with his condition.

After particularly difficult nights, Fong said, he would tell the family he wished it would all be over.

When doctors told him early last year that he likely had only four to five months left to live, he decided to pursue medical aid in dying under Hawaii’s Our Care, Our Choice Act.

For him, knowing the option was available brought relief.

“He knew that’s what he wanted and he just wanted to be at peace, with his parents and his other siblings,” Fong said.

The decision, however, was difficult for the family. Fong’s mother, who was one of her uncle’s only surviving siblings, initially opposed it and repeatedly urged him to keep fighting. The two argued over his decision, and Fong said her mother cried frequently.

Eventually, her mother came to accept that the decision belonged to her brother. Although letting him go caused her tremendous pain, Fong said, seeing him continue to suffer was even more difficult.

The family also struggled with the lack of emotional support surrounding the decision. Fong said medical aid in dying remains a sensitive and often taboo subject, making it difficult for them to openly discuss what they were going through.

Her uncle’s longtime physician helped guide the family through the process, explaining the requirements and answering their questions. Fong described the doctor as kind, caring and informative.

Her uncle ultimately died at home, in the Nuuanu house where he had grown up.

“It was a peaceful goodbye,” Fong said.

Her family’s experience comes as the number of Hawaii residents dying after ingesting medication prescribed under the state’s medical aid-in-dying law continues to rise.

Hawaii recorded 59 such deaths in 2025, a 55.3% increase from the 38 reported in 2024, according to the state Department of Health’s latest annual report on the Our Care, Our Choice Act.

The 2025 figure is the highest annual total since the law took effect in January 2019. The cumulative number of reported deaths following ingestion of the medication reached 254 by the end of 2025.

The increase comes as the department acknowledges limitations in its ability to track patients who receive prescriptions under the law, raising questions about the completeness of the state’s annual statistics and its oversight of the program.

In its 2025 annual report, DOH acknowledged that it does not consistently receive complete follow-up information about patients who receive prescriptions under the law.

“In some instances, the attending provider is not notified whether the qualified patient subsequently died or whether the prescribed medication was ingested,” the department said in a statement to the Honolulu Star-Advertiser.

It added that required follow-up forms may not be submitted or that information requested on the forms may be reported as unknown.

The department cautioned that a missing follow-up form does not necessarily indicate noncompliance. A patient may still be alive, a provider may not have been notified of a patient’s death, or family members, hospice providers or others involved in the patient’s care may not have communicated the patient’s status.

The department said its annual reporting is based on information received from attending providers.

The reporting limitations have prompted Right To Life, an organization opposed to assisted suicide and euthanasia, to call on Gov. Josh Green to commission an independent inquiry into the state’s medical aid-in-dying program.

The organization argues that incomplete reporting makes it difficult to establish the full number of deaths and determine whether safeguards under the law are being followed.

The group’s chief executive, Alisdair Hungerford- Morgan, said the state should ensure all deaths and complications are properly documented and publicly reported.

Right To Life is also questioning why the department’s annual reports do not provide detailed information about complications experienced by patients.

The attending provider follow-up form includes questions about complications following ingestion of the prescribed medication. However, the department’s annual statistics do not provide a breakdown of complications reported by providers.

The organization said the lack of publicly available information makes it difficult to determine how frequently complications occur and whether providers are consistently completing the required forms.

The department has previously reported at least one serious complication. Its 2021 annual report described a case in which the time between ingestion of the medication and death was approximately 12 hours.

Questions about oversight also have resurfaced following a Honolulu police investigation into the October 2024 death of an 88-year-old woman who had received a prescription under the Our Care, Our Choice Act.

According to police, the woman did not self-administer the medication. Investigators alleged that her attending physician administered the prescribed medication and continued doing so after she began choking and motioned for him to stop.

The Honolulu Medical Examiner’s Office classified the woman’s death as a homicide following an autopsy.

The Our Care, Our Choice Act took effect in 2019. It allows Hawaii residents who are at least 18 years old and have a terminal illness with a prognosis of six months or less to request medication to end their lives.

The process requires participation from three health care providers: an attending provider, a consulting provider and a mental health provider who evaluates the patient’s capacity to make an informed decision.

The 2025 report identified 17 counseling providers who conducted mental capacity evaluations. Of those, three were psychiatrists.

Hungerford-Morgan said the increase in reported deaths and limitations in the state’s data make an independent review necessary.

“An inquiry should establish the full number of deaths, publish the information it holds about complications and examine how suspected unlawful deaths are identified,” he said.
