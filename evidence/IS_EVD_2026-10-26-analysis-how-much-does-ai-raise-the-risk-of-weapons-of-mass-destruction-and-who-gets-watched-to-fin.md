## I. What Does AI Contribute to increase the risk of Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear Weapons?

How, if at all, does AI increase the risk of chemical and biological weapons in society? CBRN means chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear weapons. And most people worry increasingly more about the *biological* part because AI systems that improve our knowledge (the “[uplift](https://securebio.org/benchmarks/uplift/)”) about biology both let people develop novel vaccines and potentially dangerous new pathogens. Here, uplift means how an artificial intelligence model lowers the technical, financial, or logistical barriers that enable non-experts or malicious actors to design, acquire, or deploy weapons of mass destruction compared what they could do in accessing traditional internet or offline resources.

[OpenAI has said](https://openai.com/index/preparing-for-future-ai-capabilities-in-biology/) that reasoning over biological data, predicting chemical reactions, and guiding lab experiments could help people *recreate* things just as much as it could help people improve their knowledge. And that it may be difficult to tell the difference between a chemistry professor who is trying to advance research in a critical area of science and a bad actor who’s asking a large language model about pathogens and sequencing, who wants to weaponize something. The queries might look similar in the beginning between these different actors.

But any bad actor we’re talking about with chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear weapons is more than a person trying to use AI to gain knowledge — it’s a person who still has to *build* something to cause harm. And the question is, how much more does AI make it possible for them to do that than it would be possible otherwise?

A recent model [outperformed 94% of domain experts](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026) (note: not the general public) at troubleshooting virology laboratory protocols. The 2026 [International AI Safety Report](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026), chaired by Professor Yoshua Bengio of Université de Montreal (and written by more than a hundred independent experts (with an impressive advisory panel from nominees from over 30 countries)) found that AI systems *can* provide detailed information relevant to biological and chemical weapons development, matching or exceeding expert performance in some instances.

AI has already been able to predict protein structures, the way [AlphaFold predicted the 3D shape of over 200 million proteins](https://deepmind.google/science/alphafold/), and to identify protein targets within the human system that previously were not identified. Surfacing new potential protein targets within the body [introduces a risk of potential new targets for pathogens](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11094011/) that can attack them. It’s also possible, in the world of precision medicine, to imagine the [developments of pathogens that would target a single](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41576-023-00677-8) [person](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41576-023-00677-8) [or group](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41576-023-00677-8). Suppose you want to take out the leader of a country. You get a sample of their DNA, you figure out a specific vulnerability in that person, and you develop a customized pathogen that would kill literally that person. Or worse, if you could figure out an ethnic group that you wanted to target, it might be possible to do that. These are still theoretical risks, and precision medicine has not panned out to being able to develop precision cures or precision pathogens. But already, “[scientists from Stanford University and the Arc Institute used artificial intelligence to design the first genomes for viruses](https://www.georgetown.edu/news/ai-generated-viruses-bioengineering-ethics/) that are not found in the natural world. The synthetic viruses are bacteriophages, which target bacteria and, in this case, destroyed E. coli in a lab setting.” And while these target bacteria, it’s not hard to see how such developments could also be used to target human cells.

In the live class, one of your Duke Law counterparts asked what AI really adds to the mix, if it’s just resurfacing and repackaging information already available on the web, even if it’s on the dark web. And as this [study shows](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aec2657), generative design doesn’t just lower the barrier to knowledge. AI tools can inadvertently provide actionable details, including, synthesis pathways, or optimization strategies for toxic chemicals and pandemic pathogens. It can be used to automate processes including synthesis of weapons or the component parts. And it can also generate novel information, including novel potential pathogens or targets that haven’t previously been identified.

## II. How much it raises real risk, and why nobody can say

The  2026 [International AI Safety Report](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026) conceded there is still substantial uncertainty, about how much AI capabilities increase real-world risk, given, for example, the practical barriers to producing weapons, and the fact that many developers have added safeguards to try to prevent providing meaningful assistance to novices. But many developers added those safeguards because they could not rule out the possibility that novices would be substantially aided in their pursuit to create weapons with AI models.

It’s true, that to create weapons you need access to reagents or the component parts. For nuclear weapons, you still need access to enriched uranium (or the know how and ability to enrich uranium, if you’re trying to build a nuclear weapon). You still have to have centrifuges and all kinds of technology that you don’t otherwise have easy access to in order to build a lot of these things. But biological weapons, not as much so. One of the scary things about [biological weapons is that it’s possible to build a synthetic pathogen in your garage.](https://theconversation.com/an-illegal-bioweapons-lab-was-found-in-a-las-vegas-garage-its-a-warning-for-australia-275564) It doesn’t take a lot of sophisticated technology and machinery. The technology to dispense it and disseminate may be a lot harder—it may be possible to make one person incredibly sick with it, but how does a bad actor get that one person to become a vector that spreads that illness or pathogen across society?

To figure this out, let’s assume *you’re* a bad actor for a moment. Put on your bad person hat for a moment. (One of the students in the live class collects baseball hats, and I told him to imagine putting on his “bad guy” one). I want you to try to think like a bad actor for a moment and imagine you have just synthesized a super dangerous pathogen and made one person incredibly sick with it. And you want to spread that pathogen as far as possible across the globe. How are you going to do so?

In the live class we played this out for a moment, and saw that there are limitations. If you try to get it into the water system, ideally there is biosafety monitoring of those systems. If you try to spread it through that person getting onto a plane, they would have to not have visible signs of illness to get on successfully.

So how much does AI make it likely you can spread the pathogen widely? AI could brainstorm with you, although it’s not likely to directly answer how you can create a super spreader event! But brainstorming isn’t the same as producing the dangerous thing, nor spread it, and it’s why the safety reporting keeps saying nobody can yet quantify how much worse AI makes our modern safety in light of biological risks.

Which also raises the question, what is the right *choke* point in the system to mitigate against the increased risks that AI may pose for CBRN? Is it to choke the models themselves, to prevent the models from being able to talk with a person about how a pathogen would spread? Or is it figuring out how to *safeguard* and to put into place detection in water supplies, detection in aerosolized systems, and vaccines that could quickly address containment? How effective is it to detect every conversation that’s happening with an AI system?

Why are biological weapons the hardest to trace? There’s only a certain known set of chemical properties that can really harm us, and in combination we can test and trace the different reactions, and you can trace and test for them in the environment. Likewise for nuclear weapons, the materials necessary are limited. You have to have enriched uranium, and we’re pretty good at detecting and tracing when people have the isotopes that lead to enriched uranium. Similar for radiological weapons. And yet, every new biological pathogen is *unknown* until it’s detected and tested to be pathogenic. Which means that unlike chemical agents, which typically have  predictable and detectable molecular signatures, biological agents blend into the natural background of living ecosystems making them difficult to detect.

We don’t have a good and reliable way to identify every possible pathogen and mutation that could infect us. And so instead what you try to do is figure out whether there are ways you can develop super vaccines, immune system blockers, broad-based antibiotics. So it’s a race. Try to put into place as much as you can to detect, trace, and counter a biological pathogen.

Back in 2010, when synthetic biology started to be something that people talked about a lot, there was already a pretty strong [do-it-yourself community in the synbio hackers](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/do-it-yourself-biology-shows-safety-risks-of-an-open-innovation-movement/). And there are all these [community labs](https://www.communitylabs.ai/about) that exist across different populations. People with little to no background in biology have already built synthetic organisms for hobby, for craft, and for other purposes.

Some of it is interesting and performative. There was an artist in New York, [Heather Dewey-Hagborg](https://deweyhagborg.com), who wanted to put on an art show to show how you could use discarded DNA in order to create faces of people, and to just show us how little privacy we had in the modern era. So she went around and she picked up chewing gum and cigarette butts and other materials that had been discarded, took a crash course in biology, went to community labs, and extracted the DNA. Then she used some of the computer systems that existed in order to project out from the DNA different phenotypes of the person. This is what the bridge of their nose would look like, their eye color, their face, their skin color. And then she created masks that she 3D printed and these became her art installation, *[Stranger Visions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heather_Dewey-Hagborg)*. Here’s the gum, and here are the faces. It was a really interesting and provocative art experiment that shows us the stakes of modern technology in the current era.

There are high school and college groups through the [iGEM competition](https://competition.igem.org/high-school), which have created building bricks for DNA. And they have put them into a biobricks library, a [registry of standard biological parts](https://parts.igem.org/Catalog).

AI gives you the instructions, these libraries give you the building bricks, and the limitation then is how that novel pathogen gets amplified and disseminated across society.

Think about how coronavirus kept mutating to become increasingly more transmissible over time. Suddenly, the world better understands what makes a respiratory virus super-contagious. Could the combination of super-contagious with a 24-to-48-hour onset make it possible to take a dangerous pathogen and amplify it with AI? Might this happen accidentally or intentionally and lead to a dangerous pandemic?

And if you don’t have great threat detection, because the biggest risk is from biological pathogens that might hide in the natural environment, what can law and policy do to address this risk? Do you place a choke-hold on the dissemination of knowledge through AI systems? Do you put into place surveillance to see who is making those queries and then follow them using more closely?

We don’t actually know how much AI raises the risk of CBRN, partly because legal restrictions make it difficult for researchers to conduct and publish highly realistic studies. If you think about what that means, it means that the same laws meant to prevent people from building weapons also blocks perhaps one of the best mechanisms to address the risk, which is the research that would tell us whether AI makes building them easier in the first place.

“It is challenging to accurately assess how AI systems affect chemical and biological risks due to legal constraints and international treaties, as well as ‘information hazards’ – information that may be harmful to share. For example, if researchers carry out, or publish the results of, a study on AI assistance in weapons development, they may risk inadvertently violating national security laws or treaties such as the Biological Weapons Convention and Chemical Weapons Convention.” - 2026 [International AI Safety Report](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026)


The published trials on figuring out the risk levels are thin. [RAND found](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA3892-1.html) statistically non-significant or marginal enhancement of human capabilities in high-stakes fields like cybersecurity and biosecurity. Other studies have found mild and non-significant effects. In general, these are underpowered studies and we don’t have a great way to quantify the absolute risk increase.

## III. What we have tried, what we could try, and where every road leads

Suppose you *do* figure out that AI models are increasingly capable of identifying new pathogens, or solving new vaccines, or coming up with new modes of distribution of chemical or biological weapons. What would you want to do to mitigate against that risk? I’m now asking you to think like lawyers.

Legally, what do you want in place once you know a model has super-capability in biology? Try to be creative here, because we need your creativity.

Your Duke Law counterparts came up with some suggestions. You could, for example, release increasingly capable versions of AI models that are good at biology only to certain people who have been credentialed and approved to engage with them, putting your security layer at *access* to the models. There’s no reason why most of us in *this* classroom need access to an AI model that’s super-capable in biology, for example. You could say, *you can’t have access to the most capable models without security clearance*. Would that reduce threats? It might. Is it worth trying? Maybe. But remember our discussion of [open-weight models](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/open-vs-closed-ai-models-inside-my-1a0?r=1183hr)?

Also, notice that we generally do not require pre-market testing before deployment of AI models, but one proposal that people have put forward legally is to say, just like the FDA does pre-market safety and efficacy testing of drugs before they’re released, models should have pre-market safety and efficacy testing, and you could do it by domain, like chemical and biological weapons ([or use for medical AI technologies](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-09-28-regulating-ai-like-a-doctor-fda-floats-competency-based-path-for-generative-ai-enabled-devices)), and decide that it is only sufficiently safe to release to certain populations.

The June 2, 2026 Executive Order, *Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security* [calls for several actions](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/promoting-advanced-artificial-intelligence-innovation-and-security/) “[to render the federal government and critical infrastructure entities more resilient](https://www.cato.org/blog/assessing-trump-administration-executive-order-AI-cybersecurity) in light of cybersecurity and CBRN threats, included a voluntary, 30-day pre-deployment testing process during which frontier AI models can be examined for novel capabilities—affording the government additional time to ready itself and key stakeholders for a subsequent general release.”

Another student in the live class proposed legal guardrails at the *output* level of AI models, some testing at the moment the model runs up against a question where the information could be used maliciously, so that the AI doesn’t share information about potentially harmful pathogens. While this *sounds* alluring, let’s also think through the limitations of this approach. First, if you have an [open-weight model](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/open-vs-closed-ai-models-inside-my-1a0?r=1183hr), this is a guardrail layer that simply may not exist or can be easily stripped. Frontier AI labs like Anthropic and OpenAI have put such guardrails into place (when you ask certain kinds of questions, you’ll see, for example, if you’re interacting with [Fable on Anthropic and you ask questions that even come close to anything in this area, you’ll be downgraded from Fable to one of the less capable models](https://www.anthropic.com/news/improving-fable-5-s-biology-safeguards)). That’s because the guardrails kick in, detect that you are talking about an area in which the capability of Fable exceeds what is believed to be safe for the end user, and you’re kicked down to a less capable model, like Opus 5. But in the face of adversarial prompting, [these guardrails are a “speed bump”](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-nist-continuous-ai-monitoring-godel-proof/) to a determined bad actor, not an absolute bar.

Maybe you could instead use AI to figure out what the most dangerous biological threat might be and develop vaccines and treatments against them. The idea here would be to use AI in a defensive way to counter the risks of AI, and then try to contain those risks. The hope is to stay at least one step ahead of the risks:

“Big Tech firms have recently begun building frameworks to mitigate the misuse of their most capable systems, so-called “frontier” models, which are capable of advanced reasoning and problem-solving. These frameworks, such as OpenAI’s [Preparedness Framework](https://openai.com/index/updating-our-preparedness-framework/), Microsoft’s [Frontier Governance Framework](chrome-extension://efaidnbmnnnibpcajpcglclefindmkaj/https:/cdn-dynmedia-1.microsoft.com/is/content/microsoftcorp/microsoft/final/en-us/microsoft-brand/documents/Microsoft-Frontier-Governance-Framework.pdf), and Anthropic’s [Responsible Scaling Policy](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/872c653b2d0501d6ab44cf87f43e1dc4853e4d37.pdf), are used to assess and identify thresholds of risk and trigger internal mechanisms before models are released, with CBRN-related misuse ranked as a top concern. Many companies are also running red-teaming exercises and hackathons focused on CBRN risk scenarios, stress-testing their models to identify vulnerabilities and strengthen mitigation measures. These frontier model frameworks represent the first serious attempt by the private sector to align AI development with global CBRN threat-reduction objectives.” - Cindy Vestergaard, *Harnessing AI to Move from Threat to Threat Reduction*, Stimson 2026.

Another idea is to target the bad actors, thinking that perhaps there’s only a very small subset of people who would commit dangerous acts using CBRN, a manageable number that government agencies could watch, the way the FBI goes through forums and tries to determine who might be potential *bad actors* and then targets them for increased surveillance. And here, it is helpful that if you’re talking about somebody trying to release a deadly pathogen to negatively impact some or all of humanity, they are unlikely to be working *alone* (and they are unlikely to pull it off alone so they’re going to be talking and planning with somebody, and that makes them more traceable in an increasingly interconnected world). Which means increasing the amount of surveillance of communications to try to detect those people.

I want you to step back and notice something now … each of our solutions keeps leading us to one word. Surveillance. Not just increased surveillance of AI models but increased surveillance of the *population* to try to identify threats. What’s the downside of a massive increase of surveillance across society?

## IV. Laws Attempting to Address This

Lest you become hopeless, there *are* laws either on the books or pending to try to address the potential increased risk of CBRN in society. Those include voluntary safety reviews by frontier labs, restricting funding for gain-of-function research, restricting the Department of Defense from using adversarial AI models, and calling for (voluntary) internal audits and oversight inside AI companies.

[Executive Order 14409](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/promoting-advanced-artificial-intelligence-innovation-and-security/) (discussed above), mandates classified benchmarking and voluntary safety reviews for covered frontier models before they are publicly released. It coordinates CISA, the Department of the Treasury, and the Department of Defense to treat advanced dual-use AI models as critical national security risks.

[Executive Order 14292](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/05/improving-the-safety-and-security-of-biological-research/): Ends federal funding and contracts for dangerous gain-of-function (DGOF) research and high-threat life-science research conducted by foreign entities in countries of concern (such as China) or in locations lacking adequate U.S. biosafety oversight. Directs federal agencies to suspend ongoing and new applications for federally funded dangerous gain-of-function research pending updated guidance.

[FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA)](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF13197): Cyber and Artificial Intelligence Provisions in the FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA). **Pending Legislation:**

There are pending laws, too, as we head into midterm elections. That include industry-led approaches to how to (self) regulate AI models, and creating disclosure and surveillance oversight mechanisms.

October 2026, [a confidential draft AI safety bill proposed by Anthropic—widely referred to as the AEGIS Act](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/329467/20261026/anthropics-aegis-act-lets-ai-labs-pay-their-own-auditors-while-gutting-state-oversight.htm)—leaked to lawmakers and the public, sparking sharp backlash from AI safety advocates and critics who argue the rules are too lenient. The AEGIS Act would create a registry of independent evaluators under the Commerce Department, tasked with assessing frontier AI developers and their most capable models for catastrophic risk. The evaluators would be independent in name. In practice, the bill lets labs pay their own evaluators directly, as long as that payment isn't formally conditioned on the outcome of the assessment.

“The bill would let the government block a model’s release or use only if it poses a foreseeable, imminent risk of more than $1 billion in damages, or of killing or seriously injuring more than 50 people. The risk must stem from the creation or release of a biological weapon, the model evading its developer’s controls or a cyberattack. The language mirrors that found in California’s AI laws [including SB 53](https://www.politico.com/news/2025/10/04/sacramento-california-ai-rules-00594082), which was signed into law last year.” - Owen Dahlkamp, *[Anthropic quietly pitches its own AI safety bill to lawmakers](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/10/16/anthropic-ai-safety-01124654)*, Politico, October 16, 2026.

### Concluding Thoughts

So where does that leave us? We can’t really say yet how much AI increases the CBRN weapons risk in society. The research that would answer that question may violate the very treaties written to prevent the thing we are trying to measure.

Take a look at what we have built in the place of that answer: voluntary safety reviews, classified benchmarking of model capabilities, a registry of independent evaluators whom the labs themselves pay, surveillance of AI models, and then surveillance of the people interacting with the models. Every one of those is a substitute for knowing the risks itself.

That is the trade we have made, and I’m not sure we have made this tradeoff on purpose. We decided that measuring this risk was too dangerous to allow, and then we asked the companies with the most to lose to tell us how dangerous they are, and to self-regulate in case they are dangerous. In our last class on cybersecurity risks, we found that *disclosure* was our best asset if we are prepared to act on what we find, and now we face the harder problem. Which is that *disclosure* only works if somebody is allowed to look at all.
