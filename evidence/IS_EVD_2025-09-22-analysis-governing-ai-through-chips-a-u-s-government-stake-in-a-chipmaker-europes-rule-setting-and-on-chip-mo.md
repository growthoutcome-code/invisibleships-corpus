On August 22, 2025, the United States government [purchased 433.3 million shares of Intel Corporation](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/trump-says-intel-has-agreed-to-give-u-s-a-stake-in-its-company-heres-what-to-know) at $20.47 per share. With those billions of investment, the U.S. government became a 10% owner of one of America’s most important technology companies.

This wasn’t a bailout like the 2008 bank rescues. Intel wasn’t collapsing. This was the government buying ownership in a chip company at a time when controlling compute infrastructure is essential for controlling AI development.

A [PBS News article from September](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/what-economic-and-policy-experts-think-about-the-u-s-governments-stake-in-intel) quotes experts calling this unprecedented in modern times. Some see it as necessary strategic planning. Others worry about the government picking winners and distorting markets. But everyone agrees that this represents a fundamental shift in how governments think about AI governance.

*Welcome back to class. Every Monday and Wednesday this Fall you’re attending my AI Law & Policy class alongside my Duke Law students. They’re taking notes. You should be, too. And remember that this class is **85** minutes long. Take your time working through this material.*

*Want a live AI governance class with credentials for completion? Check out [Luiza Jarovsky, PhD](https://open.substack.com/users/6831253-luiza-jarovsky-phd?utm_source=mentions)‘s AI academy, [still enrolling for November](https://academy.aitechprivacy.com/ai-governance-training)!* 

Still here? Let’s dive into the second half of our module on governing **compute**.

Today we’re going to understand why owning part of Intel is relevant to governing AI, why Europe is taking a completely different approach, and why every solution to the compute governance problem creates downstream effects. By the end of class, you’ll understand not just what happened with Intel, but what it tells us about the future of AI governance. And why your roommate might be right to be paranoid about AI surveillance.

Why does compute governance matter to you? For one, your electricity bill is going up. In [Arizona, residential rates increased 8% last year, partly due to data center demand](https://azpha.org/2025/08/05/how-arizonas-data-center-boom-could-hike-your-power-bill-harm-public-health/). In Virginia, where (a disputed) [70% of the world's internet traffic passes through](https://www.vpm.org/news/2024-11-14/meta-google-amazon-dominion-energy-data-centers-virginia-power-demand), communities are fighting new data center construction because the grid can’t handle it. And if governments start monitoring chips, every AI query you make—from homework help to medical questions—could be tracked. This isn’t abstract policy. This is your daily life.

The Intel share purchase? It’s just the latest move in a global chess game where the pieces are chips, the board is the planet’s computing infrastructure, and the stakes are who controls artificial intelligence. It’s time for you to understand the game.

## **Why Everyone Suddenly Cares About Compute**

Remember [last week when we discovered DeepSeek achieved GPT-4](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/why-china-quit-us-chips-inside-my) performance with 10x less compute? Several of you emailed me and said, “*wait, so compute doesn’t matter*?” Not quite. Otherwise, why would the U.S. government buy Intel shares? Why is Europe writing elaborate compute regulations? Why is everyone obsessed with controlling chips?

The answer lies in [four unique properties of compute](https://cfg.eu/advanced-ai-technical-state-of-play/) that make it different from algorithms or data and a tempting target for regulating AI. Understanding these properties helps us understand the Intel purchase, and why [compute has been targeted as a key leverage point in AI governance](https://law-ai.org/the-role-of-compute-thresholds-for-ai-governance/).

I’m going to ask you to do some work today to make all of this come to life for you. So, pull out a piece of paper. Then draw four boxes. Let’s build this framework together. (Yes, I’m making you draw again, but I promise this helps you slow down, reinforce the concepts, and critically engage with the material. Plus you can “see” what we are talking about visually.)

### **Box 1: You Can’t Download a GPU**

In your first box, write: “Physical control is possible.”

A GPU is a physical object. It has a serial number. It sits in a specific data center. It crosses borders in shipping containers. Unlike an algorithm, which is just math, or data, which can be copied infinitely, chips are things you can control.

This is why the U.S. could ban chip sales to China. You can’t ban math, but you can ban semiconductors. The Intel purchase gives the government influence over physical chip production and distribution—something you can’t get by regulating code.

### **Box 2: Big Compute Is Traceable**

In box two, write: “Large training is visible.”

Training [GPT-4 requires about 25,000 high-end GPUs](https://medium.com/codetodeploy/a-deep-dive-into-ai-infrastructure-chatgpt-c5bb0b1cbaca) running for months, consuming 10-50 megawatts of power. That’s enough electricity for thousands of homes, generating heat visible from satellites. You need specialized buildings with reinforced floors and massive cooling systems for this kind of compute.

You can hide an algorithm on a laptop. You can’t hide a supercomputer. This visibility means governments can potentially monitor who’s training large models just by watching the power grid and heat signatures with satellites.

Think about your own community. Google “data centers near me” right now. If you live near one, your power grid is already being stressed. [Loudon County, Virginia alone supports nearly 6 GW](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/map-network-powering-us-data-centers/) of operating and under-construction data centers—more than any other U.S. county. That's power that could go to homes, schools, hospitals.

### **Box 3: FLOPs Are Countable**

In box three: “Compute is measurable.”

We can count floating-point operations (FLOPs) objectively (if you don’t remember what FLOPs are, [go back to class 7](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/why-china-quit-us-chips-inside-my) and review). The EU AI Act [set its systemic risk threshold at 10^25 FLOPs](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/high-level-summary/)—a specific, measurable number. Unlike trying to measure “intelligence” or “safety,” FLOPs give us something concrete to regulate.

Though as [our discussion of DeepSeek showed last week](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/why-china-quit-us-chips-inside-my), this metric might not measure what we think it measures. It’s like measuring a car’s quality by how much gas it uses. Sure, it’s measurable. But is it meaningful?

Still, it gives governments a number to write into law, which is more than they have for algorithms or capabilities.

### **Box 4: The Dirty Dozen Control Everything**

In your final box, write: “Extreme concentration.”

Remember [our discussion of the concentrated AI supply chain](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/why-china-quit-us-chips-inside-my) last week? It’s not really hyperbole to say that about twelve companies control global AI compute:

- Chip design: NVIDIA (70%+), AMD, Intel (now 10% government-owned)
- Manufacturing: TSMC (90% of advanced chips)
- Equipment: ASML (100% monopoly on EUV lithography)
- Cloud: Amazon, Microsoft, Google

Sit back, and take a look at your four boxes. These properties explain why governments think compute is the key to AI governance. But as we’ll see, each attempt to use these properties for control creates its own complications.

## **The Tale of Two Strategies**

The US purchase of Intel, and Europe’s approach represent two radically different theories of compute governance. Understanding both helps us see why the problem of trying to govern AI through governing compute is so hard.

In the live class, I described this as “buy your way in” strategy versus the “become a referee” strategy. As one student after class said to me, it’s more like “hostile takeover” vs. “hall monitor.” Honestly? Fair point.

The U.S. strategy is direct participation. If you can’t beat them, buy them. The government becomes a shareholder, gaining:

- Insight into global chip sales and customers
- Ability to steer domestic production
- Leverage in international negotiations

Think about what this means. When Intel negotiates with international customers, the U.S. government is effectively at the table. When Intel decides where to build new fabs, the government has a vote. This isn’t regulation from outside—it’s influence from within.

But this also presents risks of market distortion, picking winners, politicization of business decisions. What happens when AMD or NVIDIA want equal treatment? Do they need to sell equity too?

Europe faces a different reality. The [International Center for Future Generations in Brussel’s 2024 report](https://icfg.eu/new-icfg-report-compute-governance/) has the following blunt information about the numbers:

- EU’s total AI compute: \~32,000 chips
- Microsoft alone: \~1.8 million chips
- The ratio: 56 to 1

One of your Duke Law counterparts said, “*wait, Europe has less compute than a single tech company*?” Yep. It might be worse than that … like less than Microsoft’s spare GPUs.

In other words, Europe can’t buy its way to compute parity. They could purchase every European chip company and still have a fraction of U.S. or Chinese capacity. So, the report argues, they can choose a different strategy, which is to become the global referee of AI compute. Will that work? (Spoiler alert: it’s complicated).

The five interventions they recommend aren’t about building AI—they’re about governing AI built elsewhere. And betting that setting the rules matters more than owning the hardware.

Both approaches—the U.S. buying equity, Europe writing rules—stem from the same recognition that compute is the chokepoint for AI development. But their solutions reveal different philosophies about how governments should exercise power in technology markets.

## **Europe’s Five Interventions—Governing What You Can’t Build**

To understand the ICFG’s proposals, we first need to grasp the reality that shapes them all.

Europe has approximately 32,000 AI-capable chips across all its supercomputers. [Microsoft alone has 1.8 million](https://www.businessinsider.com/microsoft-gpu-targets-1-8-million-ai-chips-this-year-2024-4). That’s not a gap you close with investment—it’s a structural inequality that defines your entire strategy.

The U.S. can afford to compete everywhere. They have Intel (now 10% government-owned), NVIDIA, AMD. They can buy stakes, build infrastructure, train foundation models. Europe faces a different reality. Like a boutique law firm competing against global practices, Europe must choose its battles with extreme care.

This reality is already reshaping the European AI landscape:

- [Mistral](https://www.storyboard18.com/digital/explained-what-is-mistral-ai-and-why-is-it-seen-as-europes-answer-to-openai-80864.htm), once positioned as Europe’s answer to OpenAI, focuses on efficiency rather than frontier capabilities
- European funding increasingly flows to safety research rather than model development
- Major European corporations are licensing American AI rather than building their own

The ICFG report explicitly acknowledges this, arguing that the “EU should not attempt to compete with the US and China in training general-purpose foundation models.”

But such a strategic pivot has profound implications. When European businesses run on American or Chinese AI, they’re accepting values and biases embedded elsewhere. When foundation models become infrastructure—like operating systems or cloud platforms—Europe becomes dependent on foreign technology for core economic functions.

But if Europe can’t control what gets built, could they control how it gets used? Let’s examine each intervention through this lens, comparing it to the American approach of buying equity stakes like Intel.

**Intervention 1: Strategic Compute Allocation.** If you can’t compete broadly, dominate narrow niches

The idea here would be to redirect Europe’s limited compute toward areas where compute alone doesn’t determine success:

- AI safety and evaluation research
- Specialized scientific models where Europe has unique datasets or expertise, such as manufacturing, agriculture, and health care
- Testing and verification capabilities

This makes sense because Europe can’t outspend Microsoft on compute, but compute isn’t everything. European privacy expertise, scientific datasets, and regulatory experience provide advantages that extra GPUs can’t replicate.

But this may also better align with what people actually *want* from AI. [Today, Michael Spencer at AI Supremacy breaks this down](https://www.ai-supremacy.com/p/how-do-normal-people-view-generative-ai-autonomy-creativity-relationships), how most Americans don’t want AI doing everything. So maybe niche is better?

This strategic realignment is already underway. The European Research Council shifted funding priorities in 2024. [Horizon Europe](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-ai-research) now allocates substantial AI funding to safety and specialized applications rather than general model development.

**Intervention 2: Governing the Giants.** If you can’t build it, at least inspect it.

Here, the question is, should the EU AI Act create a new regulatory category for models above 10^26 FLOPs (10x current threshold) requiring:

- Pre-development security audits
- Physical observers during training
- Government evaluation before deployment

Think about this compared to the Intel stake. The U.S. influences compute from inside companies. Europe wants to place observers inside training runs.

But would any company accept European government observers watching their training? The U.S. might—they have equity relationships. But why would Chinese companies comply? This assumes leverage Europe might not have.

Here, draft guidelines exist, but no company has volunteered for observation. The threshold is so high (10^26 FLOPs) that it [might not apply to any models until 2026-2027](https://epoch.ai/blog/model-counts-compute-thresholds).

**Intervention 3: Enforcement by Triage.** Do more with less.

EU positions pay €50,000-60,000. Silicon Valley engineers earn $400,000+. Europe is trying to regulate technology built by people making 8x their salary.

**The Numbers Problem:**

- EU AI Office staff by 2025: \~100 people
- Expected models needing oversight: 100+
- Math: One regulator per potential AI system

**The Triage Proposal:**

- Top 10% by compute: Deep evaluation
- Middle 30%: Standard review
- Bottom 60%: Self-certification

**Intervention 4: The Foresight Unit.** Create a unit to anticipate AI developments, tracking compute trends and projecting capabilities.

This highlights the information asymmetry. The U.S. government, through its Intel stake, sees chip orders in real-time. They know who’s buying thousands of GPUs, suggesting major training runs. Europe must guess from outside.

But prediction is really hard, especially without inside information.

**Intervention 5: Multilateral Compute Oversight.** If you can’t control it alone, get everyone to agree.

Now the idea would be to create an international registry of large training runs, starting with EU-U.S. cooperation, and then expanding globally to include more participants.

This presents its own challenges. Think about the U.S. Intel purchase. The U.S. government is now both the regulator and the shareholder. Would they share intelligence that might disadvantage Intel? Would they agree to rules that reduce Intel’s market value?

And the problem, of course, is that even close allies can’t agree. [France calls U.S. proposals “digital imperialism.”](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/business/2018/01/24/merkel-macron-warn-against-perils-digital-imperialism/) The UK wants special post-Brexit arrangements. So, as of now, no meaningful international agreement exists, even though there are nice proposals like this one from the Center for Future Generations on a [Global Compute Governance Consortium](https://cfg.eu/enhancing-global-ai-governance/).

## **The On-Chip Monitoring Debate—Where Both Approaches Converge**

Despite their different strategies, both the U.S. and Europe agree on one mechanism, which is building monitoring directly into chips.

The idea is to include firmware on chips that tracks:

- Cumulative FLOPs performed
- Training vs. inference operations
- Whether thresholds are exceeded
- Reports to designated authorities

But now I want you to STOP and *think about what this means for you*. Every time you use ChatGPT to help with homework, ask Claude a medical question, or use AI for creative projects, that computation could be logged. Who sees this data? How is it stored? Could it be hacked?

Could it be subpoenaed? These are the kinds of conversations being actively discussed by policymakers.

Take the 10% stake the U.S. government has in Intel. Could they use that to push for such monitoring in Intel chips? Would Intel’s customers accept surveillance-enabled processors?

We can draw on an interesting precedent to think about this. In [2016, the FBI wanted Apple to unlock an iPhone](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/law/fbi-apple-encryption-dispute). Apple refused, won public support, and established the principle that companies shouldn’t build government backdoors.

What if the government had owned 10% of Apple’s stock. Would the calculus have changed? Would they have been able to refuse, if the government was both the regulator and largest share owner?

It would be sort of like a parent being who is on their child’s school board. Suddenly every school decision feels personal.

What you should see from all this mapping, is that while every delegation had reasonable positions, they’re mutually *incompatible*. The US wants transparency from others but exemptions for national security. The EU wants privacy protection that makes monitoring impossible. China wants access without restrictions.

And notice how the Intel stake creates novel conflicts? The government is simultaneously regulator, shareholder, and strategic competitor.

And while some scholars and commentators have compared [governing compute to regulating nuclear weapons](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/to-govern-ai-we-must-govern-compute), that’s an area that requires rare materials and complex facilities. AI requires math and electricity. So which is harder to govern?

## **Mapping the Cascade Effects**

As you think about this harder, you start to realize that every intervention that we’re imagining here would create a cascade of next order implications. Let’s trace some of these cascades systematically to really understand what happens when we try to make compute our leverage point in AI governance.

We can start with something that may directly affect you:

**Scenario 0: Your University/Workplace Implements Compute Monitoring**

**What happens next?**

- Every AI query for assignments or work projects is logged
- Students/workers self-censor, avoiding AI for sensitive topics
- Underground markets for unmonitored AI access emerge on campus
- Academic/work performance diverges between those with private access and those without
- Institutions can't tell what work human vs. AI is, grading/evaluation systems collapse

**Scenario 1: Perfect On-Chip Monitoring Implemented**. Let’s now imagine every chip reporting on training runs above 10^26 FLOPs.

What happens next?

- Immediate black market in pre-2025 chips without monitoring. Old GPUs become incredibly valuable.
- Innovation shifts to algorithms that achieve the same results with 10^25.9 FLOPs - just below the threshold. Companies hire mathematicians to optimize below thresholds rather than improve capabilities.
- Countries that don’t implement monitoring become AI havens. Training moves to international waters or space. Yes, people are seriously discussing satellite-based training to avoid jurisdiction.
- The monitoring system itself becomes a target. Imagine hacking a system that knows every AI project globally. It’s the ultimate intelligence coup.
- Public backlash ensues against surveillance. The same people worried about TikTok knowing their preferences now have government monitoring of all computation. Political parties run on ‘compute privacy’ platforms.

**Scenario 2:** The EU creates strict rules for models above 10^26 FLOPs.

- Now what’s the cascade?
- Companies simply don’t release models in Europe. The EU becomes an ‘AI desert’ where citizens use VPNs to access foreign AI.
- European researchers emigrate.
- The regulations meant to protect Europeans exclude them from AI benefits.
- Or worse - companies train models at 10^25.9 FLOPs, claim compliance, but use algorithmic improvements to achieve dangerous capabilities anyway.

**Scenario 3:** Neither the US nor China joins a multilateral registry. What happens noq?

- We get a ‘splinternet’ for AI. Western AI, Chinese AI, and everyone else has to choose sides. Developing nations become battlegrounds for AI influence, receiving ‘aid’ in the form of compute access conditional on alignment. It’s the Cold War but for artificial intelligence.

### **This brings us to some uncomfortable truths at the end of this module:**

1. **Every technical solution has already been defeated theoretically**: For every governance mechanism we’ve considered today, researchers have published papers on how to circumvent it. We’re playing catch-up to our own innovations.
2. **The pace mismatch is accelerating** DeepSeek achieved in months what took OpenAI years. Meanwhile, the EU AI Act took four years to draft. By the time any governance is implemented, it’s addressing yesterday’s technology.
3. **The Intel equity stake shows governments will take unprecedented actions** The US government owning 10% of Intel wasn’t considered possible five years ago. What else that seems impossible today will be normal by 2030?
4. **Constraints shift rather than disappear.** Control chips, and algorithms become more efficient. Control algorithms, and energy becomes the bottleneck. Control energy, and innovation moves to new computing paradigms. The constraint moves rather than submits to governance.

**Which is why in our next class,** we are going to start to shift our focus some to other mechanisms of governance, starting with Bias and Fairness in AI models, and governing the “effects” and “use” of models.
