*8:30 a.m., Monday. Welcome back to Professor Farahany’s Advanced Topics in AI Law and Policy Class. We are on week 10 of class.* 

Last fall, Meta began shipping a [$799 wristband](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/03/inside-the-lab-building-for-the-metaverse/) that reads the electrical signals in your wrist muscles before your hand moves. The device detects your *intention* to gesture and translates it into a device command. No screen tap, no voice prompt. Thought, expressed as movement before the movement occurs, becomes input.

That product dropped into the middle of a federal circuit split that was already unresolved, and made the question at the center of this week unavoidable. *When the government wants evidence of what you know, where does “your body” end and “your mind” begin?* And as the interface between mind and device collapses, does the constitutional line separating them have any remaining purchase?

Today we trace the doctrinal foundation, examining why the Fifth Amendment’s core distinction between testimonial and physical evidence was straining before smartphones existed and how digital evidence exposed the crack. On Wednesday, we will see how that crack has become a (maybe) circuit split. Then on Friday, we’ll ask what happens when the authentication interface is your nervous system.

## What the Clause Actually Protects

The [Fifth Amendment’s Self-Incrimination Clause](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-5/) of the U.S. Constitution is a single sentence: No person “shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” Three words have done the doctrinal work for over a century. “Compelled” sets the threshold of government coercion. “Witness” limits the relevant category of evidence. “Himself” makes the prohibition about self-supply rather than evidence generally.

Courts settled fairly quickly on what “witness” means. It implies testimony, meaning the communication of knowledge or belief drawn from the suspect’s mind in response to government demand. The clause protects against being made to speak against yourself, not against having physical facts about your body taken and used against you. That distinction between testimonial and physical evidence is the engine of everything that follows.

The government can compel a great deal that would incriminate you. It can take your blood, your fingerprints, your DNA, a voice recording for comparison, a handwriting exemplar. None of those implicate the Fifth Amendment. They are physical evidence, extracted from the body without requiring you to use your mind to supply information.

*[Schmerber v. California (1966)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/384/757/)* drew this line explicitly. Schmerber was arrested for drunk driving. Police drew his blood over his objection and used the results at trial. The Supreme Court held this did not violate the Self-Incrimination Clause. The privilege, the Court wrote, protects a person from being forced to prove a charge “from his own mouth,” but taking physical evidence from the body falls outside that protection. The underlying logic was about what Schmerber called the “cruel trilemma.” When you are compelled to speak, you must confess, commit perjury, or be held in contempt. A blood draw skips the trilemma entirely. You just have to sit still.

*(Worth noting that several scholars pointed out almost immediately that blood reveals your drug history, your medical conditions, your genetic information. Calling it “purely physical” papers over a substantial amount of informational content. The Court acknowledged the tension and went with the binary anyway. It would come back.)*

## When Producing Documents Became Testimony

For two decades, Schmerber’s testimonial/physical divide seemed workable. Then the IRS started subpoenaing accountants’ records, and *[Fisher v. United States (1976)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/425/391/)* introduced the concept that has driven every digital-evidence Fifth Amendment dispute since.

The IRS was investigating taxpayers and served subpoenas on their lawyers for clients’ accounting documents. The lawyers invoked the Fifth Amendment. The Supreme Court held that the documents themselves were not privileged; they were voluntarily created business records. But the Court recognized something the Schmerber binary couldn’t capture. Responding to a subpoena can itself be a communicative act, even when the documents it yields are not.

Why? Producing documents in response to a subpoena implicitly communicates three things, each coming only from the suspect’s mind. First, the documents exist. Second, they are in the suspect’s possession or control. Third, they are authentic. Each of those is an assertion, a statement of fact derived from knowledge. Forced assertions of fact derived from knowledge are precisely what the Self-Incrimination Clause is meant to prevent.

This is the [act-of-production doctrine](https://ffslawfirm.com/understanding-the-fifth-amendment-act-of-production-doctrine/). From Fisher forward, the question in any document or device case is never just “is this evidence incriminating?” The question is whether the *act of producing it* communicates something from the suspect’s mind that the government couldn’t otherwise prove.

Think of it as the difference between handing someone a stack of photographs and telling them you have a stack of photographs. The photographs themselves may not be privileged. The *act of producing them* implicitly tells the government that you **knew they existed, you knew where they were, and you are confirming these are the ones being asked about**. That communication comes from you.

## Compelled Cognition and the Hubbell Principle

*[United States v. Hubbell (2000)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/530/27/)* pushed the Fisher logic to its limit and produced the principle that your live counterparts identified, independently and correctly, before reading a single case.

Webster Hubbell was a former Associate Attorney General drawn into the Whitewater investigation. He entered a plea agreement with Independent Counsel Kenneth Starr, agreed to cooperate, and was served with a grand jury subpoena demanding eleven broad categories of documents. He appeared, invoked his Fifth Amendment privilege, was granted use immunity, and complied, producing 13,120 pages of documents across 11 boxes. The government then used those documents to indict him on tax and fraud charges entirely unrelated to the original Whitewater matter.

The Supreme Court reversed unanimously. The critical finding was that the Independent Counsel had not known in advance that any of those specific documents existed, let alone that Hubbell possessed them. Compelling Hubbell to locate, identify, sort, and assemble a responsive production forced him to use “the contents of his own mind” to supply information the government couldn’t have proven it already knew. The documents were the fruit of privileged **cognitive labor**.

The principle Fisher established and Hubbell sharpened is that the act of production involves the use of the suspect’s own mind when it requires cognitive work, searching, sorting, authenticating, assembling, that communicates implicit assertions beyond what the government already knows.

The live students connected this to the subpoena exercise before any instruction. One student working through the hypothetical wrote that figuring out which platforms hold data about you, determining which records are responsive, deciding what counts as an AI conversation versus a search query, all of that “would create friction with this Hubbell principle.” That is the right analysis. The mental labor of assembling a response is the privileged thing, not just the materials the labor yields.

Another student pushed Hubbell toward a place the Court hadn’t gone. What if the inferences embedded in the data are wrong? Attention data measures where your eyes went, not what you intended by going there. A platform might encode the inference that you were anxious about something because you re-read a particular article, when you were re-reading because the font was small. The subpoena would compel production of a cognitive portrait that never accurately corresponded to any actual mental state. Does the privilege protect against false cognitive portraits as well as true ones? There is no doctrinal answer. Keep it in mind.

## The Foregone Conclusion Limit

The act-of-production doctrine would be unworkable if it protected every compelled production. Courts developed the [foregone-conclusion doctrine](https://goldsteinmehta.com/blog/foregone-conclusion-doctrine) as a limiting principle.

Compelled production is not testimonial, and therefore not privileged, when the government can independently establish three things. (1) The evidence exists, (2) it is in the suspect’s possession or control, and (3) it is authentic. When all three are already known to the government, the act of production adds no new testimonial content.

The foregone-conclusion doctrine matters enormously for encrypted devices, which is where Wednesday’s cases begin. If an agent saw specific files before a drive was seized and locked, compelling decrypted access to those files may fall within the exception. Your knowledge of what is there is already established. If the government is fishing, demanding “the contents of this encrypted drive” without knowing what is inside, the foregone-conclusion hook has nothing to catch.

This sets up a structural paradox that digital evidence makes acute. The government must know enough about what is on your device to establish a foregone conclusion. The only way to know what is on an encrypted device is to access it, which requires you to provide the key your privilege may protect. Round and round.

## The AI Conversation as a Category Problem

The live students in the class articulated a distinction that the doctrine has not quite worked out. Several drew a line between data they had actively chosen to create for a human audience, like emails and texts, and **AI conversation logs**, which several described as thinking out loud with a non-judgmental audience of one.

One student put the distinction this way: When you have a conversation with AI, the log “captures process instead of just the product.” In a text message exchange, the drafts you deleted are gone. In an AI conversation, the whole iterative arc of reasoning, asking, revising, pushing back, arriving somewhere unexpected, is preserved. Producing it, this student argued, is closer to producing a diary or therapist’s notes than an email chain.

Another student framed it in terms of audience and intent. Emails and texts are records of completed thoughts transmitted to another person, selected, edited, and sent with the knowledge that someone else would read them. The AI conversation, by contrast, has no social audience, was written to no one, and captures cognition in process rather than communication in finished form.

Apply the Hubbell framework to that distinction. If an AI conversation log is a record of externalised reasoning rather than a record of chosen outputs, compelling its production does more than authenticate existing documents; it compels disclosure of a process the suspect never chose to address to any human. Whether that process receives Fifth Amendment protection is a question Wednesday’s cases begin to answer, and Friday’s will push further.

## Where Monday Leaves Us

The testimonial/physical divide looked clean in 1966. *Schmerber* said the body is fair game but the mind is protected. *Fisher* said even the act of producing physical materials can be testimonial. *Hubbell* said the cognitive labor of responding to a subpoena is itself the privileged thing when the government is relying on the suspect’s mind to supply what it does not already know. By 2000, the doctrine had moved a long way from “just sit still for a blood draw.”

What it had not yet confronted was the specific problem of a device unlocked by a physical characteristic of your body that, once opened, reveals everything you know. When you press your thumb to a phone, are you supplying a physical trait the way you supply a fingerprint at booking? Or are you communicating, through the act of authentication, that you know how to access the device and control its contents?

Wednesday’s cases show two federal circuits applying the same three-element test, compelled, incriminating, testimonial, to nearly identical facts and reaching opposite conclusions. The gap between them illuminates exactly where the doctrine runs out of vocabulary.
