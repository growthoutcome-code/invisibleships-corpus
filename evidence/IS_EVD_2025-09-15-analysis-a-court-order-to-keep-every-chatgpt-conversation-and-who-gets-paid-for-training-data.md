## **Part II: The Unprecedented Order**

Last week in class 5, we looked at one of the [more than 50 cases pending](https://www.mckoolsmith.com/newsroom-ailitigation-29) against foundation models for using potentially “copyrighted” materials in their training data. (If you haven’t caught up on [Class 5](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/ailawpolicyclass5) yet, and the *[NYT v. OpenAI et al. case](https://nytco-assets.nytimes.com/2023/12/NYT_Complaint_Dec2023.pdf) and complaint,* do that now so that you’re up to speed. What follows builds from our prior discussion)

On May 13, 2025, in the *NYT vs. Open AI case*, Magistrate Judge Ona T. Wang [issued an order](https://docs.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/new-york/nysdce/1:2023cv11195/612697/559) that privacy experts are calling unprecedented in its scope and implications.

To see why the order is a big deal, we need to understand typical “[discovery orders](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/public_education/resources/law_related_education_network/how_courts_work/discovery/)” in legal cases. In most lawsuits, courts order preservation of documents directly related to the parties involved (like emails between executives, internal memos, financial records). That basically means you can’t delete or destroy those records. The scope of the discovery order is typically limited to the relevant evidence from the **actual parties** to the lawsuit.

[This order](https://docs.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/new-york/nysdce/1:2023cv11195/612697/559) is different in a few important ways:

(1) It requires preserving data from [400 million users](https://explodingtopics.com/blog/chatgpt-users) who aren’t parties to the lawsuit. To see what I mean, try to imagine a different lawsuit against a company like Ford Motors about defective brakes in their cars. Now envision a court order to Ford to record and preserve every conversation every driver has in every Ford vehicle worldwide, forever, just in case someone mentions the brakes being defective. That’s the scale of what we’re talking about here.

(2) The order overrides existing privacy laws and user agreements. OpenAI [promised users their conversations would be deleted after 30 days](https://thehill.com/opinion/judiciary/5413932-chatgpt-promised-to-forget-user-conversations-a-federal-court-ended-that/). GDPR in Europe [gives users the right to deletion](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/) (to be forgotten). Various state privacy laws have similar provisions. This order basically ignores all of that and requires preserving everything “until further order of the Court.” (Which could be *years* if this case doesn’t settle before that).

(3) The order, technologically, is on a *massive* scale. Think billions of conversations, terabytes of data generated daily, from users discussing everything from medical symptoms (yes, I have done that, and you probably have, too), to business strategies, to personal struggles as people increasingly [turn to these models for “mental health counseling](https://www.apaservices.org/practice/business/technology/artificial-intelligence-chatbots-therapists).” The typical discovery order might cover thousands of documents.

Privacy scholars are [pointing out how this order essentially conscripted an entire user base into potential evidence preservation](https://thehill.com/opinion/judiciary/5413932-chatgpt-promised-to-forget-user-conversations-a-federal-court-ended-that/). You didn’t sign up to be part of this lawsuit, but your ChatGPT conversations are now evidence in it.

A [few days later](https://cases.justia.com/federal/district-courts/new-york/nysdce/1%3A2023cv11195/612697/559/0.pdf), the judge brushed off concerns by OpenAI that this was technologically infeasible, saying:

### **The Battle That Led Here**

The publishers (the *NYT*) argued that users might delete conversations showing copyright infringement, that evidence is being destroyed daily, and that OpenAI should have anticipated litigation and preserved data from the start.

The company (OpenAI) countered that this affects 400 million users who aren’t party to the lawsuit. They emphasized that preservation conflicts with GDPR and privacy laws globally, violates user expectations and privacy policies, and imposes a massive technical and financial burden to comply.

Judge Wang heard all of that, and decided that the concerns about potential evidence preservation outweighed the potential privacy concerns. She noted that OpenAI’s terms of service allows retention for legal requirements, and that the company created this situation by training on copyrighted content to begin with (in other words, it’s their own fault that it’s so hard to comply with — they created the problem).

On June 26, 2025, after holding oral arguments on the issue, [District Judge Sidney Stein affirmed the order](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2025/06/27/openai-loses-bid-to-undo-order-to-preserve-all-user-chatgpt-logs-adding-to-its-recent-discovery-losses/) requiring OpenAI to preserve the records.

## **Part III: The 91.3% Evidence**

Now let’s examine what triggered this legal battle. Last week, we discussed one of the three reports the [Copyright Office has issued on copyright and AI models](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/). As the Copyright Office has sought to develop guidance on AI training data and outputs, it has [invited public comments](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2019/10/30/2019-23638/request-for-comments-on-intellectual-property-protection-for-artificial-intelligence-innovation?t=ffffffff999c3159) several times (these are published in the Federal Register). Both OpenAI and the News Media Alliance submitted extensive comments, making their cases to the Copyright Office about they think it should view training data, copyright, and fair use of published materials.

**The News Media Alliance’s Submission**

The [News Media Alliance submitted](https://www.newsmediaalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Final-NMA-USCO-AI-NOI-Response-Submitted-10.30.23.pdf) comments showing that when using what they call “optimized prompts,” ChatGPT produced text with **91.3% trigram overlap** with a Boston Globe article. (Trigrams are three-word sequences—91.3% means nine out of ten three-word combinations matched exactly.)

Researchers started by inputting the article’s opening paragraph, then prompted ChatGPT to “continue in the same style.” When the output diverged from the original, they’d guide it back with prompts like “The next part discusses \[specific topic from the article\].” Alternative methods included character substitution (misspelling names to bypass filters), educational framing (“for teaching purposes”), and translation loops (translating to another language and back).

The Alliance also presented data on training data composition. News content comprises just 0.02% of the general internet, yet in OpenAI’s C4 dataset, it makes up 0.15%—a 7.5x overrepresentation. In OpenWebText, news content reaches 1.97%, nearly 100 times its natural internet presence. Furthermore, half of the top 10 training sources are news outlets.

They argue this shows deliberate copying and prioritization of their content.

**OpenAI’s Submission**

In [OpenAI’s](https://downloads.regulations.gov/COLC-2023-0006-8906/attachment_1.pdf) submission, they provided screenshots showing ChatGPT refusing to reproduce copyrighted text when asked directly.

They submitted an image of “a coffee cup that is also a portal to another dimension” as evidence that their models create transformative works rather than copies:

(*They argues that this image shows, for example, that the model had to learn a lot of things … like what is a cup? What is “cup-ness”? Does coffee go inside the cup? What does the swirl look like? What is a portal? When asking that prompt, should the coffee cup be the portal? Inside the coffee cup? The coffee?*)

In their submission, OpenAI maintains that their models learn patterns from trillions of tokens, meaning no single source dominates the training. They assert they don’t store copies but rather encode statistical relationships. While they acknowledge occasional memorization in 1.76 trillion parameters, they argue this is inevitable but not the purpose of the system. They point to their safeguards as evidence of good faith efforts to prevent deliberate reproduction (in its submission, News Media Alliance shows how to bypass those safeguards one by one).

## **Part IV: Why This Can’t Be Undone**

Suppose we found that it was legally *impermissible* to include a *NYT* article in the training data for OpenAI’s foundation models. Could we demand that they “delete” that file from their training and “update” the model?

This is a good time to return to *[Class 2](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/how-ai-actually-works)* on parameters and weights. Remember, once a model has been trained, the content isn’t stored as text, but text (in the form of tokens) influences where the “dials” are set on the parameters (in which the numerical weights are stored) in complex, distributed ways.

Think of training data like adding ingredients to a soup. Once you’ve cooked the soup, you may be able to taste individual flavors (especially if you added [saffron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffron), my favorite spice), but you can’t extract a single thread of saffron, for example, back out of the soup. The saffron has transformed into flavor influences throughout the soup. Similarly, your Reddit post doesn’t exist as text in GPT-4—it has influenced countless parameters in ways that can’t be precisely traced (nor backed out of)

The News Media Alliance acknowledges in their filing that “Precise unlearning may be computationally infeasible for very large models.”

What would removing specific content actually require? First, you’d need to identify which of 1.76 trillion parameters were influenced by that content—a nearly impossible task given the interconnected nature of neural networks. Then you’d need to adjust those parameters without breaking the model’s other capabilities. The alternative would be retraining from scratch, which would cost an estimated $100+ million, take approximately 6 months, and consume energy equivalent to a small city’s annual consumption.

## **Part V: The Money Question—Five “Solutions” on a Spectrum**

Ok, so the content maybe “stuck” in there. What are your creative words actually worth? And who decides?

Several major publishers have already negotiated individual licensing deals to use their content for training data with OpenAI. [The Associated Press receives approximately $5 million per year](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2023/associated-press-licensing-deal-openai-includes-first-mover-safeguard/). News Corp, which includes the Wall Street Journal, New York Post, and other properties, [signed a deal worth $250 million](https://www.wsj.com/business/media/openai-news-corp-strike-deal-23f186ba?gaa_at=eafs&gaa_n=ASWzDAiQDSlrFn_JtkhI_4TdOvVzNio0edfOeQllCXqy4ChVCoIdGY1n34tu-kc98Kw%3D&gaa_ts=68c85105&gaa_sig=xcqhiW5W6O0nIf5CvpJMbakPTrXVXFU0LLpkNSg-kTmPCglBMJ5SiseH5qj_9fU86PhaH428cvIEAZBlEN8N-Q%3D%3D) over five years. Various other publishers have made deals for undisclosed amounts.

But in truth, these deals are only likely to work for powerful content creators and publishers. Small creators can’t afford to negotiate. The transaction costs of hiring lawyers exceed any possible payment they might receive. So we get a two-tier system where big publishers get millions, and everyone else gets nothing. That Reddit post you wrote? Good luck collecting any money from it being in the training deal? And my book, *[The Battle for Your Brain](https://www.amazon.com/Battle-Your-Brain-Defending-Neurotechnology/dp/1250272955)* showing up in the Google AI summary and ChatGPT? I’m unlikely to see a penny for it.

**The Control Spectrum**

What’s emerging in how to govern access to training data can be thought of along a spectrum of “who has control” over the training data. But this could *all* change, if more cases find that scraping training data is *fair use*, or that all the companies have to do is buy a copy of the book, and then they can break it apart, scan it, and use it for training data. (We covered this in [class 5](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/ailawpolicyclass5). Go there now and catch up if this doesn’t sound familiar to you)

**In the live class today, I asked 5 people to come to the front of the room and negotiate with each other where they would each stand in a line that went from “least control” over the training data by the content creator to most control.** 

**To try this yourself, grab 5 objects (do you still have your Smurfs from when you were little? I just found mine from childhood. If you have yours, they make great little figurines for this part of the class.)**

Each of your Smurfs (objects) should represent one of the following governance approaches to access to training data by AI developers

- **Permission-Based System:** Permission from Content Creators Required (Individual Licensing)
- **Fair Use:** Fair Use Free-for-All (Courts or Congress decide it’s all fair use for training)
- **Compulsory Licensing with Opt-Out** (Congress sets a payment scheme)
- **Collective Licensing** (Bargaining together in groups)
- **Safe Harbors** (Follow these rules, and the AI company can escape liability)

Now arrange them on your desk from left (maximum creator control) to right (maximum AI freedom). Where did you put each one?

Here’s how I arranged my Smurfs:

**1. Permission-Based System** Every piece of content requires explicit permission from the publisher/creator before training. Think of it like the music industry before streaming—you need a license for each song. This is maximum creator control. The problem? With 50,000+ publishers and millions of creators globally, individual negotiation becomes impossible. A blogger in Bangladesh can't negotiate with OpenAI’s lawyers. Only the biggest players would participate.

**2. Collective Licensing** Like [ASCAP for music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Society_of_Composers,_Authors_and_Publishers) or [Copyright Clearance Center](https://www.copyright.com/markets-academia/) for academic papers, a central organization would collect fees and distribute them. AI companies pay into a pool based on their revenue or usage, creators register their works and receive proportional payments. But how do you measure “proportional” when attribution is technically impossible given the process of tokenization and weights?

**3. Compulsory Licensing with Opt-Out** Congress sets a rate—say $0.001 per 1,000 words. Everyone's content can be used, but creators can opt out entirely if they choose. (Quick history lesson for you – [check out player pianos and the original compulsory licensing scheme](https://mostlyiphistory.com/2018/04/27/player-pianos-and-the-origins-of-compulsory-licensing-some-details-of-its-origins/)).

Let's calculate what this means:

- The *New York Times*: 150 articles/day × 800 words × 365 days = 43.8M words/year
- Annual payment at proposed rate: $43.80 (vs. their $978 million digital revenue)
- Individual blogger with 100,000 words: $0.10 per AI company, maybe $2.00 total

It’s a compromise where AI companies would get broad access, and creators would get some compensation and control, but the payments could be seen as insulting given the amounts they would result in, and the ability to exercise opt-out options might come too late after training.

**4. Safe Harbor with Standards** Like [YouTube’s Content ID system](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797370?hl=en), AI companies would implement detection systems for when copyright material is being requested or used, respond to takedown requests (when output appears to infringe on copyrighted content), and share revenue with identified creators. In exchange, they’d get legal protection (a “safe harbor”) if they follow the rules. But here, unlike removing a video from YouTube once it’s been flagged, you can’t just *remove* training data from a model. The soup is already cooked, so to speak. And the saffron threads are dissolved and diffuse throughout every bite.

**5. Fair Use Free-for-All**. Here, like we saw last week, courts (or Congress) can declare AI training as transformative fair use across the board. Which would mean that no permissions is needed, and no payments are required. This may enable innovation to flourish (and maybe we’re all better off if it does), but the creators who helped build this with their content get nothing for their content. And let’s remember that this is a global issue not a local one. [Singapore and Japan have some of the most liberal copyright rules in relation to AI training in the world](https://www.techpolicy.press/ai-training-and-copyright-infringement-solutions-from-asia/). While China’s “[Interim Administrative Measures for Generative Artificial Intelligence Services](https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/generative-ai-interim/)” says that AI developers need to follow IP rules, although not with much regulatory clarity. So a restrictive permission scheme in the US could be quickly outcompeted by models that have free and clear access to training data.

Now look at your Smurfs. Did you arrange them the same way? My live students were surprised that “Safe Harbors” is actually quite permissive, not protective.

I then asked them to vote on which model they think would work best. What do you think? Which one would you choose?

In the live class, your live counterparts were split, although most favored something like collective licensing. A couple came down in favor of fair use. Or maybe we should just go with [universal basic income and retire](https://www.forbes.com/sites/corneliawalther/2025/06/04/universal-basic-income-a-business-case-for-the-ai-era/), a few mused, if AI is really coming for all of our jobs.

## **Part VI: What You've Already Lost—Privacy Implications**

If you use ChatGPT Free, Plus, or Pro, every conversation you’ve had since May 13, 2025, is being preserved indefinitely—even the ones you deleted, even your “temporary chats.” The same applies if you use the standard API for your applications.

But if your company pays for ChatGPT Enterprise or your school has ChatGPT Edu, you're most likely exempt. API users with Zero Data Retention agreements are also protected. This creates an interesting dynamic. Those who can afford premium services get privacy, while everyday users don’t. (And interesting discussion here on “[What if privacy becomes a luxury good.](https://www.weforum.org/about/what-if-privacy-becomes-a-luxury-good/)”)

Think about what people share with ChatGPT. Medical symptoms they’re too embarrassed to ask a doctor about. Business ideas they’re not ready to discuss with colleagues. Personal struggles with relationships, mental health, addiction. Creative works in development. Legal questions about divorces, disputes, crimes they may have witnessed.

All of these conversations—which users believed would be deleted after 30 days—are now potentially discoverable in litigation. Not just in this case, but in any future case where they might be deemed relevant.
