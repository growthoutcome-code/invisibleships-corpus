# Memphis data center moratorium fails after City Council vote ends in tie

[Memphis City Council](https://www.commercialappeal.com/story/news/local/2026/10/20/memphis-city-council-national-guard-ice-resolution/92353439007/) declined to pass a [moratorium on data center development](https://www.commercialappeal.com/story/news/courts/2026/10/20/memphis-city-council-bans-reversed-data-center-vote/92328876007/) in Memphis after a vote resulted in a 6-6 tie during an Oct. 20 meeting.

Councilwoman Yolanda Cooper-Sutton was not present for the vote.

Members of the crowd broke into protest after the vote, as the meeting hit its five-hour mark. The Council did pass an ordinance regulating data centers, but its contents were not immediately clear. A mid-meeting substitution made it so that members of the public — and some Council members — did not know what they were voting on.

"Councilmembers were trying to read the ordinance on the spot," Councilman JB Smiley Jr. said after the meeting.

Councilmembers left immediately after the vote, and it wasn't immediately clear what the next steps are for data center regulation.

Earlier in the meeting, Councilman Jeff Warren made a motion to hold the moratorium until the next meeting but didn't have the rest of the Council's support.

The vote followed a lengthy Oct. 20 meeting that included impassioned public comment from the audience. Council members had six data center-related items on the agenda, and the reordering of the ordinances and a last-minute substitution made the meeting difficult to follow.

The Oct. 20 meeting came two weeks after a tumultuous Oct. 6 City Council meeting [made national headlines](https://www.cnn.com/2026/10/20/us/memphis-council-data-center-vote). Protests erupted after the Council voted to punt a vote on the data center moratorium at that meeting, and the meeting was abruptly gavelled into a recess.

The Council issued a handful of bans for "disruptive behavior," and a legal battle ensued.

Nearly 40 people gave public comment on the moratorium during the Oct. 20 meeting. Many speakers told Council they were not following the wishes of the people and were putting residents at risk by letting the vote drag out.

Several speakers said Council members' stances on this moratorium could kill their chance at reelection. As one speaker put it: "If you vote 'no,' you gotta go."

At least two people were escorted out of the meeting after shouting from the audience during discussion of a resolution calling for the removal of the National Guard and U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement from Memphis. [That resolution passed 9-1](https://www.commercialappeal.com/staff/84115206007/annie-goldman/).

## Council moves forward with other data center policies

Crowd members, packed into the Council chambers, held up signs as Council discussed ordinances related to the regulation of data centers.

Council advanced two ordinances related to data center development — one which updates zoning codes relative to data centers and a second which adopts a land-use framework for data centers and other "resource-intensive uses."

Council also passed one ordinance regulating data center development, but some Councilmembers said they don't know what was in it, as Warren substituted an ordinance mid-meeting.

Council held two other data center ordinances for further discussion.

Warren also pushed his colleagues to pass an ordinance introduced earlier in the day that would establish a framework for a new board that would collect and distribute additional funds from local data centers. The board would be responsible for distributing that money, which Warren intends to be poured into initiatives that would benefit Memphis communities.

Warren raised the ordinance during the Council's Oct. 20 executive session, which was held a few hours before the regular meeting, where he seemed to have support from the rest of the board, and the legislation passed a first reading at the afternoon regular meeting.

If passed after two more readings, the City will have to go to the state legislature to ask for the authority to collect money from data centers beyond property taxes. The state would then need to pass legislation allowing the City to do so.

Warren said he has spoken to prominent members of Memphis' business community, who he said are supportive of the initiative and willing to lobby for the City in Nashville.

Warren's vision still has a few hurdles to clear — the City can't receive extra money beyond property taxes from data centers without the state passing new legislation — but he said it doesn't hurt to give it a shot.

"If we don't try, I think we're hurting ourselves," Warren said during the committee meeting.

Data centers have made donations to Memphis institutions in the past, including in-kind donations from the company now known as SpaceXAI to Memphis-Shelby County Schools, but this policy would create a contractual relationship between the City and companies with local data centers, like SpaceXAI, to pay fees based on energy usage.

Developing an independent agency responsible for distributing these funds would help maintain transparency as to where that money is going, Warren said. The ordinance authorizes the money to be used for things like neighborhood revitalization, workforce development, educational programming and other community initiatives.

## Federal judge rules on attendance bans, decorum rules

Just hours before the Council convened its regular meeting, a [federal judge](https://www.commercialappeal.com/story/news/courts/2026/10/16/activists-banned-memphis-city-council/92288310007/) lifted [bans](https://www.commercialappeal.com/story/news/politics/2026/10/15/activists-sue-memphis-city-council-ban-justin-pearson/92262492007/) the Memphis City Council served to a group of advocates who protested the board's decision to [postpone voting](https://www.commercialappeal.com/story/money/business/development/2026/10/06/memphis-council-shutdown-after-audience-erupts-over-data-center-moratorium/92058244007/) on a data center moratorium Oct. 6.

**More:** [Federal judge reverses Memphis City Council bans before data center vote](https://www.commercialappeal.com/story/news/courts/2026/10/20/memphis-city-council-bans-reversed-data-center-vote/92328876007/)

In his ruling, U.S. District Court Judge Thomas L. Parker for the Western District of Tennessee said City rules lack a provision allowing the Chair to issue prospective bans that prohibit someone from attending a future meeting. He went further to say the Council's speech and decorum policy explicitly limits any sanctions given to attending members of the public to the meeting in which a disruption occurs.

Parker also commented on the City Council's decorum policies as a whole and the "vague and ambiguous standards" that Chairwoman Jana Swearengen-Washington used when banning a handful of individuals out of the over four hundred people that attended the Oct. 6 meeting. He also said the Council's restrictions on slanderous and defamatory statements are "problematic."

Cooper-Sutton was also suspended from chairing her Council committees for 30 days. The suspension does not prevent Cooper-Sutton from attending or voting at committee and regular meetings, but she was not seen in City Hall for the entirety of Oct. 20.

## Security and protocol changes take effect

The Memphis City Council announced Oct. 19 it would be tightening security protocols for upcoming meetings, and the Oct. 20 meetings saw some of those changes in action.

**More:** [Memphis City Council has new safety measures after data center protest](https://www.commercialappeal.com/story/news/politics/2026/10/19/memphis-city-council-meeting-security-changes/92335928007/)

City Hall security could be seen enforcing a new clear bag policy and strictly controlling access into the building. Hundreds of people lined up over an hour before the Council chamber doors opened for the 4 p.m. regular meeting.

Commercial Appeal reporters noticed an increase in security officials present, including those from the Memphis Police Department.

During the meeting, security personnel were limiting access to the chambers, and a line of people waited at the door to get in. A makeshift overflow area in front of the building housed a crowd watching a livestream of the meeting.
