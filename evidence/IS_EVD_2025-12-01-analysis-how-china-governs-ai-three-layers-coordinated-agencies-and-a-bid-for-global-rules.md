# I. Two Announcements, One Week

*“China is not years and years behind us in AI. Maybe they’re three to six months.”*

— [David Sacks, White House AI Czar](https://www.cfr.org/article/china-united-states-and-ai-race), July 2025

Let’s look at the tale of two press conferences:

**July 23, 2025:** President Trump stands at a podium in Washington for his “Winning the AI Race” summit. The message: America will dominate AI through deregulation, energy abundance, and keeping chips out of Chinese hands.

**July 26, 2025:** Three days later, at the World AI Conference in Shanghai, Chinese Premier Li Qiang announces a proposal to establish the World AI Cooperation Organization—headquartered in Shanghai. The [Global AI Governance Action Plan](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/mfa_eng/xw/zyxw/202507/t20250729_11679232.html) that emerges calls for “equal rights for all countries” in AI governance and “capacity building” for the developing world.

As [Politico reported](https://www.politico.com/newsletters/digital-future-daily), “While Trump flew home early from APEC, Xi pitched Shanghai-based organization.”

Same week, same technology, completely different visions of what the “AI competition” is truly about.

Your live counterparts are starting to think that China’s position for global influence is starting to make a lot of sense. What about you?

## A. The Conventional Wisdom

The standard story on the “AI race” is that the US leads in AI because of its frontier models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) and its control over advanced chips (Nvidia’s H100s and beyond). China is “catching up,” perhaps only months behind, but hampered by US export controls that cut off access to the best hardware. The race is toward AGI (artificial general intelligence), and whoever gets there first wins.

As CFR President Michael Froman puts it in his analysis of [China, the United States, and the AI Race](https://www.cfr.org/article/china-united-states-and-ai-race): “The United States tends to define \[the competition\] in terms of the race toward Artificial General Intelligence... self-improving artificial intelligence which surpasses the cognitive power of human beings.”

There’s truth in this framing. But it misses something important.

*What if China isn’t trying to “catch up” in the race we’re running? What if China is trying to change which race matters?*

# II. What if China isn’t playing catch-up. What if it’s changing the game?

To understand China’s AI strategy, we need to see it as a series of countermoves designed to neutralize US advantages while building Chinese leverage. I want to walk you through *three* of these countermoves, because they reveal a strategic sophistication that the “catching up” narrative obscures.

## 1. Compensate (Quantity + Efficiency > Raw Power)

The US controls access to the most advanced AI chips. Nvidia’s cutting-edge processors are banned from export to China. Game over, right? Not quite. China’s response has been to compensate through sheer scale and algorithmic efficiency.

Consider [Huawei’s CloudMatrix 384 system](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/huawei-shows-off-ai-computing-system-rival-nvidias-top-product-2025-07-26/), which clusters 384 of China’s domestically-produced Ascend 910C chips to deliver approximately 300 petaflops of AI compute. For context, that’s comparable to what you’d get from roughly 300 Nvidia H100s, which would be achieved not through chip-for-chip parity, but through *architectural innovation* that networks many weaker chips together effectively.

And then there’s [DeepSeek](https://www.deepseek.com). When this Chinese AI lab released its [R1 reasoning model](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1) in early 2025, it sent shockwaves through Silicon Valley because it achieved comparable performance at a fraction of the training cost to US models. [DeepSeek reportedly trained R1 for approximately $5.6 million](https://www.reuters.com/technology/artificial-intelligence/big-tech-faces-heat-chinas-deepseek-sows-doubts-billion-dollar-spending-2025-01-27/), compared to the hundreds of millions typically spent on frontier models.

*What do you think the lesson here is? Maybe if you can’t win on raw power, you compete on efficiency? Or if you can’t access the best chips, you figure out how to do more with what you have?*

## 2. Control Inputs (The Rare Earth Leverage)

Here’s something that doesn’t get nearly enough attention. While the US controls the chips of today, [China controls the materials needed to make the chips of tomorrow.](https://www.csis.org/analysis/limits-chip-export-controls-meeting-china-challenge)

[China dominates global production of rare earth elements](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2025/10/21/despite-us-push-china-poised-to-dominate-rare-earths-for-years), which are the minerals essential for semiconductors, electric vehicles, wind turbines, and advanced military systems. [According to the International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025), China processes approximately 90% of the world’s rare earths and produces about 70% of them.

In October 2025, China’s Ministry of Commerce issued [Announcement No. 61](https://cset.georgetown.edu/publication/mofcom-notice-2025-61/), adding several rare earth processing technologies to its export control list. The policy applies a “foreign direct product rule,” which is the same mechanism we discussed in [Lecture 22](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/us-ai-policy-myths-and-realities?r=1183hr&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web&triedRedirect=true) that the US uses to restrict chips to China, but applied to rare earth separation and processing. If your rare earth technology has Chinese origins, you now need Chinese government approval to export it.

Which means this isn’t just about chips. The permanent magnets that rely on rare earths are essential for data center cooling systems, precision-guided weapons, and the electric motors in everything from EVs to industrial robots. [China produces about 94% of the world’s permanent magnets](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/19/europe-has-rare-earths-but-for-now-its-at-chinas-mercy.html).

Think of it as a supply chain chess match.

The US made the first move with chip export controls. China’s counter is to threaten the inputs that make those chips possible in the first place.

## 3. Redirect the Race (Embodied AI > AGI)

Perhaps the most important countermove is the least visible: China may be trying to shift which kind of AI matters in the overall scheme of things.

While American discourse focuses on the race to AGI, China’s investments suggest *embodied AI* is their priority instead. Which means robots, autonomous vehicles, and smart manufacturing systems, or what AI does in the physical world.

As Froman notes in the [CFR analysis](https://www.cfr.org/article/china-united-states-and-ai-race): “China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology estimates that by the end of 2025, over 60 percent of large Chinese manufacturers will have adopted some form of ‘AI + Manufacturing’ integration.” The 14th Five-Year Plan calls for “comprehensive intelligent transformation” of industrial production, AI embedded across 70% of key sectors by 2027, 90% by 2030, and 100% by 2035.

The numbers on robotics are striking. According to the [International Federation of Robotics](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/global-robot-demand-in-factories-doubles-over-10-years), China installed over 295,000 industrial robots in 2024, which is roughly half of all industrial robots installed worldwide that year. For comparison, the United States installed approximately 50,100.

Why does this matter? Because if the future of AI is less about chatbots and more about physical automation like manufacturing, logistics, agriculture, infrastructure, then China is building the foundation to dominate that future, regardless of who reaches AGI first.

In the live class, we discussed this in the context of [world models](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/glossary/world-models/), which are increasingly seen as [crucial to advance robotics and self-driving cars](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/06/11/meta-launches-ai-world-model-to-advance-robotics-self-driving-cars.html). Well, if *that* turns out to be true … is China actually *ahead* of the US in the AI race?

# III. The paradox of control at home, open to the world

Now we arrive at something truly puzzling, which is a paradox essential to understanding Chinese AI governance.

On the one hand, China maintains some of the world’s *most stringent domestic controls* on AI content. The [Interim Administrative Measures on Generative AI Services (2023)](https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/generative-ai-interim/) require that AI outputs “embody core socialist values” and prohibit content that “subverts state power” or “undermines national unity.”

Every public-facing AI model must be registered with the [Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC)](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2021-08/25/c_1631480920680924.htm) before deployment. Companies must implement real-name verification systems and maintain the ability to trace content back to individual users.

On the other hand, China has emerged as a, or perhaps *the*, global leader in open-source AI. [Alibaba’s Qwen family of models](https://huggingface.co/Qwen) has spawned over 100,000 derivative models on Hugging Face. DeepSeek’s open-weight releases [captured approximately 24% market share](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4831739-china-position-in-ai-landscape) on OpenRouter in early 2025. These models are available to anyone, anywhere, for free.

How can a government that demands ideological conformity from domestic AI simultaneously give away its best models to the world?

Let’s look at four possible explanations, all of which may be partially true:

**1. Different Rules for Different Contexts.** China may simply maintain two different regulatory tracks with one focused on tight control for domestic deployment (where content affects Chinese citizens and social stability) and, and another on permissive openness for exports (where the strategic benefits outweigh the risks). The AI models themselves may be similar, but the compliance requirements differ based on where they’re used.

**2. Undermine US Advantage.** Open-source AI directly threatens America’s business model. If Chinese models are free and nearly as capable as American ones, why would companies or governments pay for OpenAI or Anthropic? Open-source also undermines the logic of export controls, where if capable models are freely available, restricting chips would matter less.

**3. Ecosystem Capture.** The open model is the hook; the infrastructure is the lock-in. When developers worldwide build on Qwen or DeepSeek, they enter a Chinese-anchored ecosystem. Over time, they may become dependent on Chinese cloud services, Chinese tooling, Chinese updates. The model is free; the ecosystem is sticky.

**4. Standards Through Adoption (The Inverse Brussels Effect).** Remember how we discussed the Brussels Effect in [Lecture 21](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/understanding-how-the-eu-regulates?r=1183hr&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web&triedRedirect=true), of the EU exporting its regulatory standards through market power? China may be attempting something similar through technological adoption. If enough of the world builds on Chinese AI foundations, Chinese technical standards and approaches become default. Influence through ubiquity.

## The Security Question

Whatever China’s motivations, the security implications of widespread Chinese AI adoption are real and contested. Let’s take a look at what the research shows, and where the evidence remains uncertain.

**What we know:** Testing by NIST’s Consortium for the Advancement of Inferences from Scientific Evidence (CAISI) [found that DeepSeek models](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2025/09/caisi-evaluation-deepseek-ai-models-finds-shortcomings-and-risks) were approximately 12 times more susceptible to manipulation that causes the model to take unintended actions, so-called “agent hijacking,” compared to leading US models. [CrowdStrike research documented a roughly 50% increase in security vulnerabilities](https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/crowdstrike-researchers-identify-hidden-vulnerabilities-ai-coded-software/) when Chinese models are prompted with politically sensitive topics like Tibet, Uyghurs, or Falun Gong.

**What’s embedded:** Chinese models demonstrably contain content controls that travel with the weights. DeepSeek models [show approximately 45% refusal rates](https://the-decoder.com/deepseek-outputs-weaker-code-on-falun-gong-tibet-and-taiwan-queries/) for queries about Falun Gong compared to near-zero rates for US models. This is measurable in the model behavior.

**What’s alleged but unconfirmed:** Some security researchers have raised concerns about potential [“sleeper agent” backdoors](https://theori.io/blog/deepseek-security-privacy-and-governance-hidden-risks-in-open-source-ai), which is a form of hidden functionality that could be triggered under specific conditions to exfiltrate data or insert vulnerabilities. A RAND analysis explored how models could theoretically be trained to “behave differently when they are being tested versus when they are not.” But no confirmed evidence of intentional backdoors in major Chinese models has been publicly documented.

**The honest assessment:** The embedded censorship capabilities demonstrate that Chinese model developers *can* build invisible controls into model weights. Whether they’ve built more concerning capabilities is unknown. The capability is proven; the intent is debated.

This is the question policymakers face, and that you have to grapple with now. *How do you govern adoption of foreign AI when you can’t fully inspect what’s in the weights?*

In the live class we debated about whether a financial institution, who wanted to maximize shareholder value would implement Deepseek, and save money per head on the cost of AI, or if the risks would be too high.

*What would you do?* Do you think it be reasonable for the U.S. government to restrict *your* choices in high-stakes settings like hospitals, financial institutions, or critical infrastructure?

# IV. How China Governs AI

Now that you’re seeing the full picture of the strategic context, let’s do a deeper dive into the machinery of Chinese AI governance. I want to focus your attention on two elements that make China’s approach distinctive: its risk model and its regulatory architecture.

## A. Different Risk Model

Remember how the [EU AI Act categorizes risk](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/the-eu-ai-acts-reality-check-inside)? It uses *application-based tiers*: unacceptable risk (banned), high risk (heavily regulated), limited risk (transparency requirements), minimal risk (no regulation). The focus is on *what the AI is used for*.

China’s [AI Security Governance Framework 2.0](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=shihui-partners) (released September 2025 by TC260) takes a different approach. It categorizes risk by *lifecycle stage*, and asking not just “what is it used for?” but “at what stage does risk emerge?”

The Framework identifies three risk categories:

1. **Technical Endogenous Risks:** Problems inherent to the AI system itself, such as bias, hallucinations, lack of interpretability. These exist regardless of how the model is deployed.
2. **Technical Application Risks:** Problems that emerge during implementation, including supply chain vulnerabilities, infrastructure dependencies, integration failures. These emerge when you deploy.
3. **Application-Derived Risks:** Problems that emerge at societal scale, including labor displacement, social instability, erosion of human cognitive capacities. These emerge from widespread adoption.

This isn’t just academic taxonomy. It reflects a different theory of where governance should focus. The EU asks: “Is this a high-risk application?” China asks: “At what stage in the AI lifecycle does this risk need to be addressed?”

## B. The Regulatory Architecture: China’s 3-Layer Cake

As the [Shihui Partners analysis](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=shihui-partners) explains, China’s AI governance operates through a 3-layered system, which is different from the layered approach we saw with the EU governance model.

**1. Foundational Laws:** The [Cybersecurity Law](https://digichina.stanford.edu/work/translation-cybersecurity-law-of-the-peoples-republic-of-china-effective-june-1-2017/) (2017, amended 2025), [Data Security Law](http://www.npc.gov.cn/englishnpc/c2759/c23934/202112/t20211209_385109.html) (2021), and [Personal Information Protection Law](https://personalinformationprotectionlaw.com) (2021) establish the baseline. These aren’t AI-specific but create the legal infrastructure AI regulations build upon.

**2. Targeted Administrative Regulations:** Rather than one comprehensive AI law like the EU AI Act, China has issued focused regulations addressing specific technologies as they emerge: [the Administrative Provisions on Algorithmic Recommendation Services](https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/algorithms/#gsc.tab=0) (2022), the [Administrative Provisions on Deep Synthesis Internet Information Services](https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/deep-synthesis/#gsc.tab=0) (2023), and the [Interim Administrative Measures on Generative AI Services](https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/generative-ai-interim/#gsc.tab=0) (2023).

**3. Technical Standards:** Documents like [Framework 2.0](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/10/how-china-views-ai-risks-and-what-to-do-about-them?lang=en) are non-binding but influential. They shape industry practice and often get incorporated into mandatory rules later.

The Chinese term for this approach is “[small-incision regulation](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/key-amendments-to-chinas-cybersecurity-law)” (小切口立法) are targeted rules for specific technologies rather than comprehensive legislation. As [Qiheng Chen explains](https://asiasociety.org/policy-institute/chinas-emerging-approach-regulating-general-purpose-artificial-intelligence-balancing-innovation-and) in the Asia Society analysis: “Rather than attempting to legislate broadly, China has introduced targeted rules to address specific technologies or risks. This enables iterative learning, piloting, and gradual refinement.”

## C. “Agile Governance”

Framework 2.0 explicitly endorses “agile governance” (敏捷治理) as a core principle. The idea is that AI evolves too quickly for static regulation. Instead, governance should be adaptive: monitor risk changes continuously, refine classifications as technology develops, and update preventive measures dynamically.

In practice, this means Chinese regulators can move faster than their Western counterparts, but that companies face more uncertainty about what rules will apply next year, or next month. Compare this to the EU AI Act, which took years to negotiate and won’t be fully implemented until 2027 (or who knows, [based on the current simplification package](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/understanding-how-the-eu-regulates?r=1183hr&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web&triedRedirect=true)). The Chinese approach trades predictability for responsiveness.

## D. Who Regulates What

Unlike the [fragmented US system](https://nitafarahany.substack.com/p/us-ai-policy-myths-and-realities?r=1183hr&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web&triedRedirect=true) (where AI governance is scattered across the FTC, SEC, HHS, DOD, and dozens of other agencies with overlapping and sometimes conflicting mandates), China’s regulatory structure is hierarchical and coordinated:

- **[Cyberspace Administration of China](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2021-08/25/c_1631480920680924.htm) (CAC):** Lead authority for AI governance. Handles algorithm registration, security assessments, and content oversight.
- **[Ministry of Industry and Information Technology](https://www.google.com/search?client=safari&rls=en&q=Ministry+of+Industry+and+Information+Technology+china&ie=UTF-8&oe=UTF-8) (MIIT):** Industrial AI, manufacturing integration, infrastructure.
- **[State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR)](http://en.nim.ac.cn/node/647):** Competition, consumer protection, market conduct.
- **[Ministry of Public Security](https://www.mps.gov.cn/n2255079/n6865805/n7355748/n7355818/c8040661/content.html):** AI-enabled fraud, cybercrime, public safety.
- **[National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC)](https://en.ndrc.gov.cn):** Infrastructure investment, data centers, computing resources.

Strategic direction comes from the State Council and Party leadership. The agencies implement. This isn’t accidental fragmentation (as in the US) or deliberate separation of powers (as in democratic systems), but hierarchical coordination by design.

# V. The Global Governance Bid

China’s AI ambitions don’t stop at its borders. The July 2025 Action Plan represents a systematic effort to shape global AI governance—particularly in the Global South.

## A. What the Action Plan Proposes

The [Global AI Governance Action Plan](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/mfa_eng/xw/zyxw/202507/t20250729_11679232.html) contains 13 points organized around several themes:

- **Equal participation:** “All countries, big or small, strong or weak, rich or poor, are entitled to participate in AI governance.”
- **Capacity building:** Commitments to help developing countries build AI infrastructure and expertise.
- **UN-centered multilateralism:** Positioning international institutions (not US-led coalitions) as the proper venue for AI governance.
- **The Shanghai organization:** A proposed World AI Cooperation Organization headquartered in Shanghai to coordinate global AI standards.

As NBR scholar [Ren Xiao notes](https://www.nbr.org/publication/chinas-approach-to-ai-development-and-governance/), “Although the Chinese government’s position is that AI should be a tool for global collaboration rather than geopolitical competition, many observers view China’s AI development through the prism of U.S.-China competition.” A Global Times editorial highlighted that the US plan “includes further tightening of export controls on AI chips to China” while arguing that “the United States remains trapped in a zero-sum mind-set.”

## B. The Global South Strategy

Here’s where China’s open-source strategy connects to its governance ambitions. Consider the choice facing a developing country that wants to adopt AI:

- **Option A (US models):** Pay significant licensing fees for proprietary models. Depend on American companies for updates and support. Navigate complex US export control rules.
- **Option B (EU approach):** Adopt comprehensive regulations modeled on the AI Act. Build institutional capacity for conformity assessments and market surveillance. Accept significant compliance costs.
- **Option C (Chinese models):** Download free, capable open-source models. Receive technical assistance and infrastructure investment through Belt and Road partnerships. Adopt “flexible” governance frameworks.

From a purely economic perspective, Option C is often the most attractive—especially for countries without the resources to pay American prices or build European regulatory capacity.

This is the Global South strategy in action: make Chinese AI the path of least resistance for developing countries, and governance influence follows technological adoption.

# VI. The Honest Limits

I’ve presented China’s strategy as sophisticated and coherent. It is. But intellectual honesty requires acknowledging where the strategy faces real constraints and where optimistic projections may not materialize.

## A. Will AI+ Work?

The centerpiece of China’s domestic AI strategy is the “AI+” initiative, of embedding AI across 90% of the economy by 2030. But as Carnegie scholar [Scott Singer argues](https://carnegieendowment.org/emissary/2025/09/ai-china-90-percent-economy-why-wont-work?lang=en), there are reasons for skepticism.

China successfully executed “Internet+” in the 2010 by digitizing payments, e-commerce, and government services. But AI integration is fundamentally different. As Singer notes: “A hospital can install WeChat Pay in an afternoon. It cannot simply ‘install ChatGPT’ and transform medical care.”

AI integration requires sustained R&D investment, specialized talent, organizational change, and tolerance for failure and capability building that takes years.

The investment numbers also raise questions. Chinese AI venture capital funding dropped approximately 50% year-over-year in Q2 2025, reaching its lowest level in a decade (approximately $4.7 billion). Some of this reflects global trends, but some reflects specifically Chinese dynamics—regulatory uncertainty, geopolitical pressure, and competition from state-backed entities.

Singer’s broader concern: “Renewed strength breeds renewed control impulses.” As AI becomes more powerful and economically important, the Party’s instinct to control it may intensify—potentially undermining the dynamism that made Chinese tech competitive in the first place.

## B. The Chip Gap Is Real

Despite China’s compensatory strategies, the hardware gap remains significant. Analysis from the Institute for Progress estimates that when adjusted for manufacturing yields and actual deployment, China’s effective advanced chip capacity is 170-180 times smaller than that of the US and its allies.

The yield rate difference is dramatic: TSMC’s leading-edge fabs [achieve 60-80% yields on 3nm processes](https://www.tomshardware.com/news/analysts-estimate-tsmc-n3-yields-between-60-and-80-percent), while SMIC’s most advanced domestic production reportedly achieves [5-20% on inferior 7nm](https://www.techinsights.com/blog/chinas-smic-plays-7-nm-card) technology. More chips don’t help if most of them don’t work.

## C. Workarounds Have Limits

The [WSJ investigation](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/china-ai-nvidia-chip-access-6a4fa63d) we read for today, showing how Chinese AI companies accessedg 2,300 Nvidia chips through Indonesian data centers, reveals both the ingenuity and the limitations of workaround strategies. Yes, it shows export controls can be circumvented. But it also shows dependence: Chinese companies are going through elaborate third-country arrangements to access American technology because they can’t replicate it domestically.

This is a vulnerability, not a solution. US authorities could tighten enforcement. Partner countries could face pressure to close loopholes. The cloud-access model depends on connectivity that could be disrupted.

# VII. Three Theories of Victory

We’ve now spent several classes examining how the EU, the US, and China approach AI governance. Let me offer a framework for comparing them, not “who’s winning” but “what game is each trying to win?”

- **The EU has concluded it probably can’t win the technology race.** It doesn’t have the scale of the US market or the centralized coordination of China. So it’s playing a different game: be the regulatory standard-setter. The Brussels Effect means that even if European companies don’t build the best AI, European rules will shape how AI is deployed globally. Theory of victory: Set the floor everyone must meet.
- **The US is betting on continued technological leadership.** American companies build the frontier models. American chips power the training runs. American capital funds the research. If the US stays at the technological frontier, it can export models and restrict competitors’ access to hardware. Theory of victory: Be the frontier everyone depends on.
- **China is trying to make itself unavoidable across multiple dimensions.** Control the rare earths needed for chips. Dominate embodied AI and physical deployment. Flood the world with free open-source models. Build infrastructure relationships with the Global South. Even if China doesn’t win the AGI race, it’s building leverage that matters regardless of who reaches AGI first. Theory of victory: Be unavoidable across dimensions.

Which means asking “who’s winning the AI race?” may be less useful than “what race do we want to be running?”

If you think the future of AI is AGI, as disembodied superintelligence, then the US-led race to frontier models is the race that matters. If you think the future is AI embedded in physical systems transforming manufacturing, logistics, and infrastructure, then China’s embodied AI investments may be more consequential. If you think the future is determined by regulatory standards that shape global norms, then the EU’s approach deserves more credit than it typically receives.

All three might be partially right. The “AI future” probably includes all of these dimensions. Which is why the governance choices each jurisdiction makes, and how those choices interact, will shape not just who “wins” but what kind of AI future we collectively create.

So perhaps, and just hear me out here, perhaps the EU, the US, and China aren’t just competing to reach the same finish line first. Perhaps they’re each trying to *define* what counts as winning, and their governance frameworks reflect those different objectives.
