Statement 01/2026 on Smart Glasses 
Adopted on 13 October 2026 
The European Data Protection Board has adopted the following statement: 
1  Introduction  
1 As smart glasses become widely available and more frequently used, they create new 
opportunities for communication, assistance, and innovation, while also raising significant 
questions for the protection of personal data and the rights of individuals. This statement sets 
out the EDPB’s preliminary reflections on the privacy implications of smart glasses, with 
particular regard to their capacity to process personal data in ways that may be difficult for 
data subjects to perceive or control. 
2 For the purposes of this document, smart glasses are defined as wearable devices which often 
look like regular glasses or sunglasses, and which incorporate computing capabilities and 
features such as cameras, speakers, microphones, and software or applications, including 
artificial intelligence systems for various purposes (e.g. translation or object identification). 
They may connect to the internet, external devices, or mobile applications to enable additional 
functionalities. They may be operated through voice command or other input technologies, 
such as wristbands detecting hand gestures.  
3 Where smart glasses capture, collect, record, adapt, store, or otherwise process any 
information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person, they process personal data, 
and the processing is therefore subject to the GDPR1. 
2  Use Cases and Specific Risks of Smart Glasses  
4 Functionalities vary between smart glasses on the market. Common use cases include 
listening to music, making or receiving a phone call, taking photographs or recording videos, 
accessing real-time translation services, and live streaming. Some smart glasses may also 
provide ‘augmented’ experiences by displaying information to the wearer.

5 These functionalities may be used, for example, as a hand-free tool for a wide range of needs 
and in various personal or professional contexts, such as for communication, navigation, 
disability aid (for example by providing audio assistance and real -time hazard awareness), 
documentation of events, or the creation of content for social media platforms 2 . New 
functionalities continue to emerge as the technology develops.  
6 However, some of the most common uses of smart glasses, particularly recording, live 
streaming, and the sharing of footage online, may give rise to specific and significant concerns 
for the protection of personal data3, and also other rights and freedoms4 such as the rights to 
privacy, security, freedom of expression and freedom of movement.  
7 This is due to the way in which smart glasses are worn and operate. Unlike many other 
recording devices, they allow a near invisible or hidden collection, storage, and other forms of 
processing of personal data since they can be difficult to distinguish fr om ordinary eyewear. 
Therefore, they can facilitate the non-authorised recording of data subjects. In addition, some 
smart glasses may have advanced microphones, which can record background conversations 
to which the user is not participating. In addition, their possible use together with facial or voice 
recognition systems raises unprecedented questions regarding societal choices and poses a 
high risk to the rights and freedoms in public spaces. 
8 Even if the device could be identifiable as smart glasses, individuals may still oppose being 
filmed or recorded, including because they are uncomfortable with the possibility of this taking 
place5. This could also result in a subtle social effect, where individuals alter their behaviour, 
suppress spontaneity, and conform to social norms perceived to be more socially acceptable 
due to their perceived threat of unvetted surveillance.  
9 Users of smart glasses should always ensure that they respect the privacy and dignity of other 
individuals, and that they do not undertake any actions that could cause prejudice to them with 
their smart glasses. Explicitly informing other individuals of the use of smart glasses is relevant 
in this respect. 
10 The use of smart glasses may also be especially sensitive in certain contexts, such as in a 
medical facility, schools  and other places where minors are present, changing rooms, 
swimming pools or beaches, toilets, or other private or intimate situations. Use in the 
workplace may raise concerns about employee monitoring and the confidentiality of the 
entity’s information (including trade secrets and intellectual property). In such situations, 
recording or audio capture may be inappropriate, or prohibited under national law or local 
rules6.

3  General Considerations for Compliance with Data 
Protection Law  
3.1 Identification of the controller   
11 Depending on the circumstances, responsibility for processing personal data through smart 
glasses may arise for different actors, including the user of the smart glasses, the producer of 
the device, or the provider of a linked application or service (including AI systems). In some 
cases, one actor may act as sole controller; in others, two or more actors may qualify as joint 
controllers. The assessment will depend on who determines the purposes and means of the 
processing7. 
12 The qualification of the user under the GDPR depends on their use of the smart glasses.  
13 The user of the smart glasses is not subject to the GDPR, provided that the user’s actions are 
carried out in the course of a purely personal or household activity, outside any professional 
or commercial context (household exemption8). This is the case, for example, when the user 
shares a photo or video with a family member or a friend only9, or uses the device for personal 
translation in a private context.  
14 This exemption must, however, be narrowly construed10 and be considered on a case-by-case 
basis in light of the circumstances of the processing. For example, it would not apply if the 
photo or video is shared to an indefinite number of persons on a public social media account11, 
or if smart glasses are used in the course of official, professional, political or other non-
personal activities. In these situations, the user would qualify as a controller or joint-controller 
and therefore has to comply with all of  the obligations foreseen by the GDPR, notably 
transparency.  
15 In any case, the user who is recording voice or images with their smart glasses might be 
subject to other laws. For instance, some uses may be subject to criminal law.  
16 It is important to note that the GDPR does still apply to controllers or processors which provide 
the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities 12, such as 
the producer of the smart glasses or the provider of a linked application or service (including 
AI systems). 
17 The producer of the smart glasses or the provider of a linked application or service (including 
AI systems) may be considered as independent or joint controllers, where they determine, 
alone or jointly, the purposes and means of the processing. This is notably the case when the 
provider of the AI functionalities of the smart glasses processes personal data in order to

3.2 Transparency  
20 Compliance with the transparency requirements of the GDPR14 presents a major challenge in 
the context of smart glasses. Transparency is essential to make data subjects, whether they 
are users or bystanders (also referred to as ‘non- users’), aware that their personal data are 
being processed, and to what extent. 15 Such information is a necessary precondition for 
individuals to be able to exercise their rights as data subjects. 
21 The discreet nature of smart glasses means that non- users may be unaware that personal 
data relating to them is being processed 16, by whom it is carried out, and for what purposes . 
Some functions, such as translation services or use of AI features, may also be particularly 
invisible to bystanders.  
22 The current state of the art with regards to indicators on smart glasses leave many non-users 
unaware that their data is being processed.  
23 Traditional transparency notices, which can be used to inform users and usually contain 
information about the data processing, do not seem sufficient or appropriate to inform non-
users in the context of smart glasses. This is particularly true when the controller is the 
producer of the smart glasses or the provider of a linked application or service (including AI 
systems), and they process personal data relating to non- users, since they have no 
relationship with these persons and are not able to contact them.   
24 Given the current state of the art, controllers and producers should consider combining a broad 
range of transparency mechanisms when designing and developing smart glasses, including 
measures that provide by default in- the-moment transparency that processing is taking 
place17.as well as providing the required information under GDPR 18. Measures relevant to

• Visual or LED indicators – To be effective, such indicator19 should be visible in many 
lighting conditions and sufficiently noticeable to attract the attention of individuals 
within the field of collection. It should be activated long enough to make sure individuals 
understand that the smart glasses are recording or capturing  images/video for 
processing by an AI assistant. The indicator could function in different ways depending 
on the functionality being used (for example: a prolonged activation when recording 
images or sound; flashing when a picture is being taken; etc.), and it should not be 
possible to record information when the indicator is covered.  
• Audio indicators – Audio indicators and voice activation requirements (i.e. requiring 
the user to dictate the command out loud) could also increase the awareness of 
bystanders, as used on other photo or video devices (for example: a click sound when 
a picture is being taken20).  
• Smartphone based proximity notification–  Several mobile applications detect 
nearby smart glasses by recognising broadcasted Bluetooth identifiers associated to 
glasses manufacturers. Currently this type of signal can only be used to detect nearby 
glasses (whether they are recording or not), but  device manufacturers could also 
consider sending specific signals when devices are recording, therefore allowing 
nearby users to be notified. In any case, such notifications should not be the sole 
method of providing transparency, as this would exclude individuals who do not have 
such an application active at all times, and also because it should always be possible 
for an individual to accept or refuse to receive such a notification on their device. 
Additionally, this application should be designed in a way that it cannot lead to the 
tracking of the smart glasses’ users. 
• Design of the smart glasses – In addition to indicators when functionalities of the 
smart glasses are activated, other clues in the design of the glasses could be 
envisaged to help non-users distinguish smart glasses from regular glasses21.  
25 Whenever compliance with the transparency requirements of the GDPR relies on technical 
measures, it is necessary to ensure that they are effective, impossible or difficult to circumvent, 
and updated whenever necessary.  
26 The EDPB recommends the development of industry-wide standards and awareness raising 
campaigns to ensure that individuals identify smart glasses and understand what type of data 
processing may be occurring in different situations.

27 Any processing within the scope of the GDPR must have a valid legal basis under Article 6(1) 
GDPR22. Given the complexities and variations of data processing via smart glasses, the 
validity of any chosen legal basis is likely to require a case -by-case assessment taking into 
account the local applicable laws, the specific processing and its context, and any further use 
of the data.  
28 Consent under article 6(1)(a) GDPR may be the legal basis of the processing, where the 
conditions of Article 7 GDPR are fulfilled23. Some national laws protect the right to one’s image, 
which requires obtaining consent of a person for the reproduction and public distribution of 
their image: in these situations, consent will likely be the most appropriate legal basis for the 
processing.  
29 However, where the controller is the producer of the smart glasses or the provider of a linked 
application or service (including AI systems), and they process personal data relating to non-
users with whom they have no relationship, it would be difficult to obtain their freely given, 
specific, informed and unambiguous consent as required under Article 7 GDPR. 24 Consent 
obtained after collection, for example following the recording, would not be valid as the legal 
basis must exist prior to and during any processing.  
30 Reliance on Article 6(1)(f) legitimate interests as a relevant legal basis would require a careful 
case by case analysis in light of data subjects’ rights, freedoms, and reasonable 
expectations25. Considering the nature of the data, the context of the processing and the 
current insufficient transparency, which negatively impacts the reasonable expectations of 
non-users26, sufficient mitigating measures will need to be put in place in order to achieve a 
fair balance between the interests involved. This would be particularly difficult to demonstrate 
for further processing for commercial purposes that go beyond providing the service explicitly 
requested by the end-user, or for AI training27, which bystanders wouldn’t expect, especially if 
they are not aware of the initial data collection. Even if the conditions of Article 6(1)(f) are met, 
controllers would also need to ensure that all data subjects are able to object 28 at any time, 
which can prove difficult in many cases.
